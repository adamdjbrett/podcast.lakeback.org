# Episode 01: The Eagles Return to Onondaga Lake, Part 1

## Introduction

## Conversation

**[01:08] Patricia Nolan Vogt:** My name is Patricia Nolan Vogt. I'm a descendant of the Onondaga Nation. I'm sitting here today with Tina Thomas and Sarah Shute.

**[01:17] Tina Thomas:** My name is Tina Thomas. I'm from the Onondaga Nation Eel Clan. I reside on the Onondaga Nation, and I'm partnering up with Sarah.

**[01:29] Sarah Shute:** Hi, my name is Sarah Shute. I'm from the Lafayette Tully area. I'm a graduate of SUNY ESF with a background in environmental science, and I spent several years working with Tina at the Skä•noñh Great Law of Peace Center.

**[01:44] Patricia Nolan Vogt:** Growing up, I'm gonna talk about the connection to the lake that I had in my younger years. And honestly, it wasn't much. I wasn't taught much about the lake except for I remember it smelling. I remember it being an awful smell. And my father unfortunately had been in a boarding school and was pretty shut down on his educating me. As I grew up, I was educated more by my family the importance of the lake to the Confederacy, the s-story of the Peacemaker, and knowing that was the birthplace of democracy, and I think learning about the importance that it not only should have for the Confederacy, but honestly everybody, that importance that is the birthplace of democracy should matter. Tina, what is your connection?

**[02:41] Tina Thomas:** Growing up on the west side of Syracuse, I really had no connection. We just knew the lake was toxic and nobody ever went over that way. Stay out of the water. We always talked about the creek that goes right through Syracuse, Onondaga Creek, and we were told never to go in there, that it was dirty water. And it wasn't until I was in my late 30s that I found out more about the lake, and it wasn't until my 40s that I found out that this area is the home of democracy. Which ended up being more important to me than the Salt City. Being known as the Salt City is actually the first resource taken from the lake, which is really depressing.

**[03:27] Tina Thomas:** All the vegetation that you see around the lake is all new from 1929 is when they replanted after they took the first resource. So just learning more about it, all the different animal life, all the different beings that are coming back and living around the lake. The one thing that I know that still hasn't been seen is an eel. And being an Eel Clan, that's important to me because I know that an eel is the first animal to leave the water, toxic waters, poison waters. So if you don't see an eel, then you know it's still not clean.

**[04:07] Patricia Nolan Vogt:** How about you, Sarah?

**[04:08] Sarah Shute:** I would say growing up in the Tully Lafayette area, I felt very disconnected from the lake. I don't think we talked much about it other than there was a general knowledge that the lake was the most polluted lake in the world. And it's interesting, in being from those towns, the headwaters of Onondaga Creek originate in the towns of Tully, Itasco, and Lafayette, and that's one of the primary sources of Onondaga Lake, and yet there was a serious disconnect there.

**[04:41] Sarah Shute:** I would say when I really first started understanding or becoming more aware of Onondaga Lake and the serious problems there was when I was working for a small environmental consulting firm and we were hired to do some ecological studies in and around the lake, and I became much more aware of the mercury contamination and the long and really terrible and unjust history of what happened to not only the waters but the land surrounding the lake. I worked on a study. Actually, we were looking at snapping turtles in Onondaga Lake and we collected... Actually it was tissue samples, but the tissue came from the toenails of the snapping turtles. So we caught snapping turtles and clipped their toenails, and those samples were sent to a lab for analysis. And not surprisingly, there was a significant-- the contamination in the snapping turtles, which are bottom feeders, was sig-significantly higher than turtles from other surrounding wetlands that were in a much better condition.

**[05:49] Patricia Nolan Vogt:** Wow. I remember, I wanna say it was at least 10 years ago, I would walk on Onondaga Lake Park, and I remember seeing dead fish, and it was-- it was hurtful. It was-- I was sad. It-- This beautiful lake and the whole shore was dead fish. And I think that probably was my first realization of how this had an impact on the animals, especially the fish.

**[06:19] Sarah Shute:** I would say that when I began that work, the snapping turtle study and other ecological studies, I was actually quite surprised, to be honest with you, at how beautiful the lake was. You spend a lot of years being told this is the most polluted lake in the world. And you're right, you do see fish carcasses and garbage and things surrounded, surrounding the lake. It felt and feels very disrespected. And when I started working on the lake, I was really caught off guard and surprised and yet astounded at the beauty of the waters, the reflection. And so here I was participating in a study that was looking at and investigating the extent of toxic contamination in the lake, and yet it was one of the most beautiful places I had ever been.

**[07:11] Patricia Nolan Vogt:** Absolutely. Tina, what was your path back to the Onondaga Nation and how did it lead to you working at the Skä•noñh Center?

**[07:21] Tina Thomas:** I would have to say, I guess the longhouse is where my questions came in because being in the longhouse, you don't really ask questions. You observe, and your questions come afterwards. So after ceremonies, then I would ask somebody, "Where do I learn more?" And really never getting a solid answer because nobody wants to talk. It's strange, it was strange to me. And I had married Virgil Gahet Thomas and he was one of the leaders. He was a Hoyane. And he's the one that started trying to teach me language. After he had passed and I started cooking, and I've been cooking for years, but then I really looked towards traditional cooking, and that's what brought me into the Skä•noñh Center.

**[08:14] Tina Thomas:** Somebody asked me to cook some traditional foods, and then I met Sarah, and she says, "Yeah, we're looking for someone to come to work," and I said, "Give me the application." And I filled it out, and so I've been there and been a part of the Skä•noñh Center ever since. And Sarah had le- hel- led my way to learning, actually showing me how to learn. Doing just YouTube, seeing extra speakers, and even helping me with my language. And we learned that together. And everyone would say, "Not everyone is supposed to learn the language," and I disagree with that. But Sarah helped me a lot to find out who I am.

**[08:56] Patricia Nolan Vogt:** Beautiful, Tina.

**[08:57] Sarah Shute:** I could say just a few words also, although I have many to say. But Tina made me feel like it was okay to learn more, to really be able to embody these beliefs and these values into my own way of life. And I would not, and do not even to this day, I would not feel as comfortable or as confident in talking about what I've learned, history, culture, values, without Tina by my side. And whether it's about the information that we're discussing today or anything going on in my life, when I need that boost of confidence and when I need a reminder of who I am and the good work that I can do in the world, Tina is the person that I call. So our relationship is very mutually beneficial, and I could not have done the work that I've done in the last few years without her by my side.

**[10:06] Patricia Nolan Vogt:** I love this. Sarah, you've talked a little bit about the work you did as an environmentalist. What-- how did it lead you to the Skä•noñh Center and working with Tina?

**[10:17] Sarah Shute:** So after I left the job in environmental consulting, I worked on some temporary wildlife studies in New York and in the Northeast, primarily focused on birds, although much of my work in the past centered around turtles. And those jobs were seasonal, and so I came to a point where I was at a break in work, and actually right around that time, my mom became very ill, and I needed to take care of her. And so for the better part of a year, I was caring for my mom through cancer, and after she passed I was in a time of transition, self-reflection. You feel like time stands still, but really the world keeps moving. And during that time, I had learned that what we had grown up knowing as Saint Marie among the Iroquois or the French Fort at Onondaga Lake had been renovated, restored with a new narrative and new information that focused on the Haudenosaunee and Onondaga history and culture.

**[11:22] Sarah Shute:** And so I thought, "I would really like to go out and see the new center." And I was totally blown away, quite honestly, from the first signage when you walk in the door that talks-- briefly introduces you to the Peacemaker, but also the importance of Onondaga Lake, the history of Onondaga Lake, and what it looked like when it was a healthy water body and a healthy ecosystem. And I will tell you that I spent hours there reading every word of every plaque, of listening to every word on every video, and I felt like everything that lives in my heart or that I have-- that I believe is how a human being should live in this world and how we should live together as a society was reflected to me in the information at the center.

**[12:20] Sarah Shute:** And coincidentally, that day when I left the center and came full circle through the gift shop, I saw a sign on the counter that said, "Looking for assistance, please inquire within." And I applied for the job, and shortly after, I was a new center attendant and became an educator there.

**[12:39] Patricia Nolan Vogt:** We were very fortunate to have you there.

**[12:42] Sarah Shute:** Thank you.

**[12:43] Patricia Nolan Vogt:** When you both worked at the Skä•noñh Center, how did you talk about the lake? I know that there's many that go through that door. How did you start talking to everybody?

**[12:55] Tina Thomas:** I think that's where I would start, by having everyone look at the lake and all its surrounding vegetation, the trees, everything growing. We definitely focused on how terrible everything was, and then I would talk about the democracy, what this place really meant. And when any Haudenosaunee person puts their feet on the ground around the lake, we can actually say our ancestors might have stood in the same spot. A lot of people don't understand the importance of the lake. And I think that's something that really needs to be pushed out there.

**[13:42] Patricia Nolan Vogt:** I agree.

**[13:42] Tina Thomas:** And yeah, and pulled away from that Salt City that everyone calls us and start remembering this area as the home of democracy. You have democracy because of the Haudenosaunee people, and people need to learn that in school. And I think that's how we started when we started our touring. We would just try to make people understand how important a place, our place is, where your feet are. I think that's how I always just started it, was all the resources that were took, but the original plan for this place is democracy.

**[14:20] Sarah Shute:** Yeah, and I would agree. It was really Tina's idea to bring folks outside before we started a tour to say, "Look at this lake, because we're about to tell you a part of the history that happened here." And the layout of the Skä•noñh Center and the order of exhibits is very intentional and very powerful because it brings you through first the most fundamental belief system and values, which starts with the Thanksgiving Address, and we can talk about that more as we move through our discussion today. And as you continue through the exhibits, one of the final exhibits that Tina and I really focused our tour around is the coming together of the Great Peace at Onondaga Lake.

**[15:12] Sarah Shute:** When Tina and I would finish our tours, we would do that at the exhibit about the Great Law of Peace coming together at Onondaga Lake. And so we would recall to the beginning of the tour where we stepped outside and saw that actual water body and that actual place where those events happened. And it's a place where it's truly an example of people who were once in great conflict using the power of the good mind, which is using reasoning and our words and our good thoughts and our good hearts to come together to resolve conflict and to figure out a way to live together peacefully. And so the power of that was really felt there at that time in that exhibit. And so we were able to say, "Remember where we started this tour? Right outside those doors. This is where that all took place, and it's an example for you here today, and it's an example really for the entire world."

**[16:15] Patricia Nolan Vogt:** You both were there when the eagles first arrived. This, and I know this story, I've heard it many times from both of you, but I want you to share with everybody, what did you actually see that very first time?

**[16:29] Tina Thomas:** It took a while for us to even get to that because in 2020, Sarah and I were still working, and we would go, and that's when we started listening to Ganohé:nio, the Thanksgiving address, every morning, and we would burn our medicine and just give our thanks. And then we started seeing the eagles come around more. And my first experience watching the eagles build a nest is I always thought they picked up broken sticks off the ground, and I watched them rip the top of the dead trees off, the branches from the top of the trees, and break them, and then that's what they use. And we could watch them go across the grasses by the water, and they would put their talons in there and grab grass.

**[17:21] Tina Thomas:** And so they weren't going on the ground to get these bits and pieces. They were finding them, and it was deliberate what they were taking. And the thing is, we started our Ganohé:nio. And it just so happens, coincidence, I say not. I believe that the eagles were listening to our Ganohé:nio with us and felt safe to have their nest at the Skä•noñh Center.

**[17:48] Sarah Shute:** When I first came to the Skä•noñh Center, I started noticing red-tailed hawks in the area and eventually realized that there was a pair of red-tailed hawks that had built a nest in the conifer trees. The spr-- there are some spruce and fir trees in the parking lot of the Skä•noñh Center, and for a couple of years the red-tailed hawks were nesting there. In 2020 when the Skä•noñh Center was closed during the COVID closures, Tina and I were still coming to the center and working on some maintenance projects, putting together some education programs. And that's when Tina encouraged me to come outside with her each morning and listen to the Ganohé:nio.

**[18:32] Sarah Shute:** And what I really loved about that process is that I was the one who would be more anxious about meeting deadlines and getting programs finished up, and I would lose sight sometimes of what was really important. And so Tina would ask me to come outside, and I would have a hard time breaking myself away from being at the computer. And Tina, like many other times, knew probably what I needed more than what I thought I needed. And so she would bring me outside each day for fresh air and to look at the lake and to be in the sunshine and to feel the breeze on our faces. And we would just both sit in silence and listen to the Ganohé:nio.

**[19:25] Sarah Shute:** And that is when we started noticing the eagles coming to the center. And so we noticed them perching at the place of the red-tailed hawk nest. They were no longer nesting there. And they started spending more time there perching. And then we started noticing the adding of the sticks and the nesting material. And we were very hopeful. I was, as always, cautiously optimistic. And sure enough, and I believe it was 2022 was the first nest- the first nesting attempt with eggs. We had our first bald eagle nest in recorded history on the shores of Onondaga Lake, and it happened to be at the Skä•noñh Great Law of Peace Center, where we recount the story of the Great Law of Peace with the eagle placed at the top of the great white pine tree, the Tree of Peace.

**[20:22] Sarah Shute:** And that eagle serves as a guardian of the peace with its keen eyesight. A bald eagle has incredible eyesight, can see from hundreds of feet, meters away. And that eagle is there to serve as sort of a sentinel, to watch over the people and to watch over the peace and to look for threats from without and from within the community, and to remind us that we need to protect the peace and that we need to always be working to guard the peace and to keep it active, to keep it alive. Peace doesn't just remain without putting work into it, and that was the reminder, I think, that we felt.

**[21:09] Patricia Nolan Vogt:** Tina answered my next question, which was why do you think the eagles chose that spot by the Skä•noñh Center? And, in her response she does not think that it was by coincidence. I'm interested to ask you, Tina, do you feel the same way?

**[21:26] Sarah Shute:** Absolutely. A thousand percent.

**[21:28] Patricia Nolan Vogt:** I agree too. What does it mean to you personally to watch the eagles raise their young at a lake with this much history of harm?

**[21:38] Tina Thomas:** It's amazing to watch all of that happen. We have to remember how poison the lake really is. And so our concern was actually the fish that they were eating, hoping that the eggs made it through to hatch, and just keeping an eye on things like that. I was, like, the first nest with the two babies, we wrote down every day, this is what's happening at the nest, because nobody was protecting them. There were people going under the tree, over to the tree, trying to get a picture. We've even seen people try to climb the tree.

**[22:18] Patricia Nolan Vogt:** Jeez.

**[22:18] Tina Thomas:** It was something that I had to take the push, and Sarah and I just stood up to everybody and said, "These birds need help, protection." And so we did our due diligence, I think. And it caused a lot of hair on people's necks to stand up. But the first two eggs both survived. The birds grew, but before they could fledge, one of them had left the tree and was on the ground for a few days. And that's the same year, 2022, when the hawks up to SU had, had passed from the bird flu up there, and that's what they said the baby had, the one that was on the ground.

**[23:04] Tina Thomas:** But the other one that was up in the tree, that one survived, and I believe is still flying around here and should have the white head and white tail by now. And it's just funny to me that this year, the baby that was born was being watched by three eagles, and they all had the white head and the white tails. And in my mind and my gut feeling is that's their first baby that came back to help them care for this one.

**[23:34] Sarah Shute:** So for me, I certainly was worried about their safety and their protection from human disturbance. It was upsetting to see people driving up right to the trunk of the tree and trying to get photographs. I think people are mostly well-meaning, but sometimes when you're well-meaning, you can still cause harm. They certainly attracted quite a following of photographers, and I was quite worried about how their activity would impact the nest. But I've got to tell you that I was really pleasantly surprised that we, Tina and I, forged relationships with a number of the photographers, and they became very protective of the eagles and making sure that people were not getting too close to the nest, that the photographers were staying at a safe distance.

**[24:23] Sarah Shute:** They sent copies of their photos to us. They con- you know, they contacted us through social media or via text or phone call, and they would share with us so many of the updates of what was going on. And here we are in 2026, and many of the same photographers are still there taking pictures, and they know these birds intimately. So that's been a really fun thing to watch, but also a heartwarming-

**[24:51] Patricia Nolan Vogt:** It's amazing.

**[24:51] Sarah Shute:** ... experience. But yeah, also concerned about the contamination in the lake. I know there's been considerable effort at cleanup. Water quality has improved, but the fact is that the, the sediments are still laden with toxins. And yeah, I worry about the fish that they're eating, right? There's a bioaccumulation, so as the larger predators in the food chain are eating the smaller organisms, those toxins, you know, bioaccumulate or they become, they can be more-- There have been considerable efforts to improve the quality of the lake and, but the fact is there still are toxic sediments.

**[25:36] Sarah Shute:** So yes, I was also concerned about the toxicity of the lake and what the eagles might be ingesting through the fish that they were catching. Those chemicals can bioaccumulate, and so the more fish that the eagles eat, the higher concentration of contaminants in their tissue. So I worried about that then. I worry about that now. But I guess the bigger picture of what I was seeing, whether it was through the eagles nesting in such a busy, high-traffic area, exposure to lots of human activity, traffic, and them just ingesting toxic chemicals, the thing that I was noticing or the thing that was really on my mind and on my heart was their resilience, right? That even though eagles can be very sensitive to human activity and disturbance, that these birds were still nesting here, that they were still thriving, that they were having successful nests. And so to me, the takeaway was resilience, which is something that we talk a lot about also at the Skä•noñh Center.

Education that you're willing to give out to our next generations is so important, and I appreciate both of you.

**[27:11] Sarah Shute:** It's really been a privilege, quite honestly, a life's privilege for what I experienced at the Skä•noñh Center, what I have learned there, how I have been able to share that wisdom with so many other people, for the friendships and the relationships that I have, and most especially for my dear friend Tina. I wouldn't wanna have my life without her.
