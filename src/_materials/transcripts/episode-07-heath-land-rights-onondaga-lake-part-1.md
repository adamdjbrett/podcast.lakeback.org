**[00:29] Adam DJ Brett:** You are listening to Healing the Sacred, a podcast from the American Indian Law Alliance about restoring Onondaga Lake through story, ceremony, and public scholarship. The producers of this podcast would like to acknowledge with respect the Onondaga Nation, Firekeepers of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy, the Indigenous peoples whose ancestral and unceded lands include Onondaga Lake.

**[00:55] Adam DJ Brett:** Joseph J. Heath has served as general counsel for the Onondaga Nation since 1982. In September 1971, during his first week of law school in Buffalo, the Attica prison rebellion began. He later became one of the four lawyers representing the plaintiffs in the Attica class action. In this episode, Heath discusses the planning and preparation that went into the Onondaga Nation Land Rights action, how it moved through the United States court system, and where the case stands now before the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights. And now, introducing your host, Betty Lyons Hill, a proud citizen of the Onondaga Nation Snipe Clan. She is the executive director of the American Indian Law Alliance.

## Conversation

**[01:53] Betty Lyons Hill:** My original name is Kainhiah, which means small sky. My colonized name is Betty Lyons Hill, and I am a proud citizen of the Onondaga Nation Snipe Clan, and I am here welcoming today Joe Heath, who is general counsel for the Onondaga Nation. Welcome, Joe.

**[02:11] Joseph J. Heath:** Thank you. It's good to be here.

**[02:13] Betty Lyons Hill:** We are so grateful to have you today and your decades and decades of work and dedication to our nation, to our people. It's really been amazing to work alongside of you. I know that your journey started back in 1980. And so, I wanted to start there. I wanted to start with your journey in becoming general counsel for the Onondaga Nation, and how did that come about? And if you could talk a little bit about your amazing history, just prior to becoming general counsel for the nation.

**[02:43] Joseph J. Heath:** My history really begins in September of 1971. It's 55 years since the Attica Prison rebellion, and I was in my first week of law school in Buffalo, and it obviously changed my life. And I am still working on Attica. I went to a presentation about it last week. But there I worked... First of all, I learned to work with people and listen to the Attica brothers and learn from them rather than the kind of education I was getting in law school, which was pretty different. There, I worked on several criminal trials, one with Bill Kunstler and Ramsey Clark being the defense attorneys, and I established a pretty good relationship with Bill.

**[03:25] Joseph J. Heath:** Then in the early '80s, Dennis Banks, who was one of the founders of the American Indian Movement, came to Onondaga seeking sanctuary because he was avoiding a federal fugitive warrant from South Dakota coming out of the Wounded Knee takeover in 1975. Bill Kunstler was his lawyer in South Dakota, and he asked me to be local counsel for Dennis. And so that started a year and a half or so where I would come onto the nation to meet with Dennis, come into the longhouse repeatedly, and act as a liaison between the council and the outside governments that were chafing at the bit to come in and invade and arrest Dennis, which they did not do.

**[04:07] Joseph J. Heath:** Dennis decided to go back to South Dakota. A number of the Onondaga leaders and I went there twice. And a little while after that, a number of the chiefs came to my office in downtown Syracuse and asked me if I would work for them. At first it was almost no work, but that's how it started. Prior to that I had made one trip into Akwesasne by boat in 1979 because the troopers had shut the road down. I've just been lucky to be in the right places at the right time. That's what I keep understanding.

**[04:38] Betty Lyons Hill:** I love that. This story, especially the Attica story and how long it took you to even get some kind of reparation for the folks that had gone through that, and then how it brought you to working for us, I think that's a story that should be told and heard by people. So moving on to when you started working for us, I wanted to ask you, who do you think was the most influential in your work for Onondaga, teaching you more about our folks and how you would see the US courts, what that battle looked like? If you could talk a little bit about the history of how that came about to how you became general counsel for us.

**[05:17] Joseph J. Heath:** Certainly I had the benefit of a number of elders at Onondaga, but clearly the primary mentor that I had was Irv, Chief Irving Powless Jr., who throughout the '80s patiently would sit with me and point out to me how I was not understanding sovereignty and how I was still trained to think about a white lawyer and courts being the answer to everything. And just innumerable times I remember going to his house and that pool table which was actually a filing system, but in any event, Irving is the reason that I have some understanding of sovereignty today. And the patience and the wisdom that he shared with me over at least a decade before I think I began to understand sovereignty was just invaluable and precious. There's no other way to describe how much I appreciate Irving.

**[06:13] Betty Lyons Hill:** He is truly missed, for sure.

**[06:15] Betty Lyons Hill:** He really is.

**[06:16] Betty Lyons Hill:** I was thinking also about when we decided to put in our land rights action and if you could tell our listeners how that decision came about, because for us, as you know, it became-- It was illegal for a long time for us to practice our ceremonies. There was a federal law against all of that. And so the fear of our people, to make that decision to finally say, "Okay, we're ready to take that step, to try to regain some of our lands back." You know, if you could explain to our listeners how that kinda came about and the difficulties that you faced.

**[06:53] Joseph J. Heath:** Well, obviously we were monitoring the progress of the other nations' land claims. So Oneida had been pending at least 20 years, and so it really began significant discussions in the longhouse around 1990. And decades of discussion went on in the longhouse. And the elders, who probably were more accurate about what would happen than I was at the time, said, "You can't, we can't go to US courts. They will insult us. They will throw us out." And we spent a lot of time going over the problems that indigenous nations had in US courts. And we were encouraged by the progress made both in Oneida, which by then had been up to the Supreme Court I think twice, and Cayuga, which was proceeding.

**[07:45] Joseph J. Heath:** And so we were able to continue to talk about, the only way to exercise treaty rights was to bring an action under the Federal Intercourse, Trade and Intercourse Act and to try to hold the United States responsible for failing to live up to treaty rights. And at the time we filed it, the courts had made very favorable decisions in the other nations' land rights actions. I remember multiple conversations in the longhouse back and forth, a number of the elders saying that how much they distrusted US courts. We finally got beyond that, but towards the end as we were beginning to file, and we filed March 11th, and we had drafted the complaint repeatedly and redrafted it and accommodated wishes of the chiefs and the clan mothers.

**[08:36] Joseph J. Heath:** And then they said, "Well, you have a really nice legal document there, but you've left something out." And that's when they dictated the first paragraph of the land rights action to us, which was profoundly wise, both in terms of the cultural importance of how to frame the case, but in terms of how the courts responded to it afterwards. And we can probably come back to the content of that, because I think it fits later on in some of the other discussions we may have. But the point really was that, after decades we had a very special day at the nation, and we filed a complaint in the Northern District of New York. We had some really great public and media support.

**[09:19] Betty Lyons Hill:** It felt like a great day.

**[09:21] Joseph J. Heath:** It was a great day. Unfortunately, 18 days later, the Supreme Court knocked the legs out of our lawsuit and actually the right for any Haudenosaunee Indigenous nation to get any justice for treaty violations. And that came in the decision of the Sherrill-- City of Sherrill versus Oneida. The city, Oneida, had repurchased land that had been stolen from them.

**[09:48] Joseph J. Heath:** And it's important for our listeners to understand that the federal courts have had two extensive historic hearings about New York's illegal taking of Haudenosaunee land. There was a period from 1788 to essentially 1790 when New York just went out and consciously violated federal law and federal guidance and took land from Oneida, Onondaga, and Cayuga almost sequentially using the same dishonest tactics, signing with people they knew were not leaders, knowing that the federal restriction against states coming, taking Indian land were pending. And so Oneida had purchased land within their Canandaigua recognized reservation. And so they said, "We're not paying taxes, and we're not gonna live according to your rules and your zoning."

**[10:45] Joseph J. Heath:** And they did very well with that in the Second Circuit. And then it went up to the Supreme Court, and we were monitoring that case. I remember meetings in the school's library where we were preparing people for the filing of the case. And so we were carefully monitoring what was being argued in Sherrill and thought they had a really good chance. And then the Supremes issued the Sherrill decision, an eight to one decision written by Ruth Bader Ginsburg, that essentially meant that none of the Haudenosaunee land rights or land claims could survive in federal court. I remember reading that decision, my wife and I were visiting her sister in Arizona, and the way you got things quickly then was by fax, and I remember reading it page by page out of the fax machine saying, "That's the end of our case. They've just denied any justice to Onondaga and the Haudenosaunee." So that created a period of litigation in the federal courts, which we may or may not get into.

**[11:46] Betty Lyons Hill:** So going back to, instead of calling it a [land claim](https://www.onondaganation.org/land-rights/), that decision that was made, if you could tell the listeners why it was called the Land Rights Action versus a land claim and the purpose of that.

**[11:59] Joseph J. Heath:** Well, one of the mistakes that was made in the Oneida land claim was made by out-of-state lawyers. So this is decades into the litigation. It's th- that case lasted 40 years before it was dismissed. And an out-of-state lawyer said, "We're just not getting enough legal leverage here, so what we're gonna do is we're gonna up the ante, and we're going to individually sue every landowner in our claimed territory." What a colossal political mistake that was, because it ginned up massive resistance, both in local people. And when you're doing work that's as much cultural and political and diplomatic as it is legal, you have to learn to make correct political decisions that sometimes are not consistent with your legal strategy.

**[12:56] Joseph J. Heath:** And that was just one of the worst mistakes ever. I've seen a few in the 51 years I've been at this. And so that resulted in both hostility, which was clearly not helpful, because ultimately, if there is going to be justice for all the land theft, and that's-- we're still working diligently on that, it has to be diplomatic. We're not gonna get any, any justice out of US courts. The courts responded to that by eventually ruling that neither Oneida nor Cayuga could get the relief of having any title to land actually transferred back to them. And that meant that their cases, which were on return of land, had a much more untenable basis. But it also led the Onondaga leadership to direct us not to call the case a claim, because it wasn't.

**[13:53] Joseph J. Heath:** We weren't claiming the land. It was an in their rights to the land and all of the land that was stolen from them. Their right to have a ruling from the federal courts that the federal law had been violated, but, more fundamentally, their right to be heard on environmental matters within their homeland, and particularly around Onondaga Lake, as at that point, the Superfund lawsuit was just gearing up and the nation was being excluded from that, and that was one of the primary motivations. The nation declared, "We have a right to be heard when you're making environmental decisions about our lands and our waters." And what really delineated the case from the prior land claims was the first paragraph that the council dictated to us, that's because it called for a healing between the nation and all others, and explained the unique spiritual, cultural, historic relationship of the land, which is embodied in the Great Law of Peace, which was founded right around the corner from where we're recording here on sacred Onondaga Lake.

**[15:03] Joseph J. Heath:** And it proclaimed that the relationship of the Haudenosaunee people and the Onondaga to their land and waters goes far beyond legal concepts of ownership, and explained that the duty of the leaders was to work for a healing of the land to protect it and to pass onto future generations. And then declared that they brought the actions-- and then declared that they brought the action on behalf of its people in the hopes that it will hasten the process of reconciliation and bring lasting justice, peace, and respect among all of us. And so they extended a hand of friendship and peace to all of their neighbors rather than suing them. And that's what delineated the [land claim](https://www.onondaganation.org/land-rights/) from the land rights. Unfortunately, the courts never understood that distinction.

**[15:54] Betty Lyons Hill:** How, h- what was their exact response?

**[15:58] Joseph J. Heath:** Well, their exact response was to dismiss the case shamefully. That's the short version of it. It took a few years. But, ultimately, after the Sherrill decision in the Second Circuit, which is the intermediate federal court between the Northern District here and the Supremes, the Second Circuit used Sherrill to sequentially dismiss first Cayuga in July of 2005, then Oneida in 2010, and then the Onondaga case, eventually in 2013. I got to argue the appeal in the Second Circuit on Columbus Day in 2012.

**[16:36] Betty Lyons Hill:** Wow, that's profound.

**[16:37] Joseph J. Heath:** It was,

**[16:38] Betty Lyons Hill:** Yes.

**[16:38] Joseph J. Heath:** it's very appropriate. And it was not lost on the Onondaga people. By then we knew what the decision would be because it was cast in stone given the prior rulings that I just talked about. In any event, we had 10 minutes, that's all, and within one week they issued a one-page decision dismissing our case, affirming the dismissal by the Northern District. Then we had to apply to the Supreme Court for the right to be heard. It's called certiorari. It's a administrative step one must take, and of course that was denied late in 2013. But the good aspect of all of that was that we had exhausted our domestic remedies. That's a phrase or a concept used in international law. And as I said before, back when we were in the longhouse talking, I was told repeatedly, "We don't belong in US courts. We belong in an international forum." And that's where we were able to go after the Supremes turned us down.

**[17:40] Joseph J. Heath:** And so we filed a petition in the Organization of American States Inter-American Commission on Human Rights, and we charged the United States with three human rights violations for allowing the land to be taken illegally by New York, for allowing the land to be environmentally destroyed, particularly the sacred lake, which was turned into the most polluted lake in the country, and for having a court system that gives no remedy for treaty violations. And that case continues today. About four years ago, we achieved a historic administrative step in that case. Essentially we survived a motion to dismiss. It's called a ruling on admissibility, where the commission ruled that our case was strong enough to continue until they make a ruling on the merits.

**[18:33] Joseph J. Heath:** And that's the First Nation in Turtle Island that has achieved that status against the United States. We're confident from the rulings there and from the law in that commission that eventually the commission will rule in our favor. Unfortunately the United States' main defense was it doesn't matter what happens in this forum because you can't make us a- adhere to any ruling you make. The United States refuses to recognize the jurisdiction of the Inter-American Court on Human Rights, which is next probably down the hall from the commission, but it would have teeth. And we know from the experience of the Dann sisters and the Western Shoshone who won a decision against the United States as individuals using treaty rights, that the United States simply ignored the decision afterwards.

**[19:27] Joseph J. Heath:** And so that's what they say. "We will not abide by any ruling of this international commission." The United States is really an outlier in that it will not, has not accepted the jurisdiction of any international court. At the same time, this important petition has helped the nation continue with its never-ending battle to achieve land justice and to get more land and waters returned, and to have their jurisdiction over their homelands recognized.

**[20:03] Betty Lyons Hill:** Before I have you turn toward the lake and focusing on the lake and the things that have been happening since then, and the cleanup and all of this work that you've been doing, I would like you to kind of explain to those listening what happens then once the Organization of American States renders a decision. You know, we have a lot of people that come up to us and ask, "How come you don't take this to the United Nations?" They ask these questions out of ignorance to the process of how these things work. And if you could explain that, because we get that question asked and posed to us all the time.

**[20:38] Joseph J. Heath:** Well, it's interesting because, as I do some work with traditional Cayugas, and there's an effort there to work with the United Nations and the Special Rapporteur on Indigenous rights. But what needs to be understood is that the Special Rapporteur has very limited, has no jurisdiction over the United States, and whatever she or he said would be ignored as well. There is no court that we could take the United States to. If there were, we would've filed there. And so given the incredible racism of the colonial courts, and that's the only way to re- that you can accurately describe what US courts have done to deny Indigenous nations' land rights. And that's because all of the land on Turtle Island is stolen Indian land.

**[21:27] Joseph J. Heath:** And it's just not, at least so far, it, there isn't the capacity within the United States political or legal system to admit that historic harm. We don't get healing or reconciliation, which is not a word Onondaga uses that much anymore, because what are we reconciling to? Does that mean that everybody will go back to Europe? Maybe. But in any event, we have to use diplomacy in order to properly exercise treaty rights. And that's what's been effective for Onondaga. It led to the return of the 1,000 acres. And so until we get a ruling from the international forum, perhaps we can bring that back into US courts and change US Indian law.

**[22:17] Joseph J. Heath:** There's a good book by a friend of mine, Peter d'Errico, called "Federal Anti-Indian Law." And it's something that I lecture on in law schools when asked. And what it means is that they have just made up a separate set of rules that only applies to Indian nations, and essentially, particularly for the Haudenosaunee land rights actions, means the nations cannot seek any justice in US courts. Because there are two elements to the dismissal of the Onondaga case that we need to understand. The court said if the harm that you're seeking redress from is old, and of course the treaties are all more than 200 years old, and if the court concludes that your request will upset the settlers, then you lose. Well, I think most people don't need much explanation for what that's gonna result in.

**[23:12] Joseph J. Heath:** And it's important to understand that when they made that ruling in Sherrill, there was no proof whatsoever about any disruption. And going back to the question about how our case was different from the other nations, we were careful not to be disruptive. We called for healing. And after the Sherrill decision, we submitted over 1,000 pages of affidavits. Most of those were from lawyers and law professors about how the courts have been closed. But I submitted an affidavit that said, "This has not been disruptive." We have favorable headlines in the newspaper. We have people cooperating with us. Because of the wisdom of that first paragraph, the case was not disruptive. It was supported. It still, I think, enjoys broad support, and yet the courts threw it out.

**[24:00] Betty Lyons Hill:** I thought that it also addressed the issue of laches. I mean, even in that first paragraph about the disruption, it kind of touched on that really. We weren't allowed to bring anything to court for a very long time. It was illegal to even be heard. And then when they went ahead and changed their federal law to say that we could now all of a sudden be who we were, and how long it took us to bring the case to the court. It's logical as to why it took so long, and I think that it reminds me kind of what the Onondaga County Executive is doing right now without trying to give a piece of our sacred lake back, right? It's that they're gonna find any excuse to dismiss.

**[24:43] Joseph J. Heath:** Well, that's exactly what happened in the land rights. And yes, that's what's going on now. We submitted a great deal of proof about the fact that the courts were closed. None of this mattered whatsoever. We never got a day in court. We had a couple hours argument way early in the case in Northern District. But what the Second Circuit kept saying was, "None of this matters. There is a rule that you can't bring these cases, and we will not look."

**[25:13] Joseph J. Heath:** And there's never been proper litigation about, well, why did you wait that long? And all the other factors that they just pulled out of thin air. And then as they successively dismissed the cases, Cayuga and Oneida and Onondaga, they kept trying to justify this bogus rule, knowing, clearly knowing that it was indefensible. And so they said, "Oh, well, it's not really laches," and all of this is under the umbrella of equity. This is an equitable defense they declare that we're handing the states against the Indigenous nations. And it breaks every fundamental rule of equity. We scratched our heads for years trying to get them to understand that.

**[26:00] Joseph J. Heath:** And it-- what it means is that the normal rules of law and equity do not apply to Indigenous nations or peoples when it comes to the US courts. It is absolutely shameful. Going back to Attica, I've had 51 years of practice in US courts with some very alarming racist situations. This law shocks even me, and it's, it's something that we're gonna change one way or another because the Onondaga people will never give up working to regain their land and their stewardship. And as long as I'm breathing, I'm gonna do what I can to help.

**[26:39] Betty Lyons Hill:** And you have. I've had the honor and privilege of watching you work for three decades. I've been working for the nation for nearly three decades. 29 years, and I've watched the tirelessness in which you've approached all of these things, even when some of us have wanted to just say, "Okay, I guess that's it," you know. You keep regenerating the fight in all of us too. We're never gonna give up. You're never gonna give up this fight. Thank you so much, Joe. I can't tell you how grateful we are that you took the time out today. You are often-- I tease about this. You're texting and emailing at 4:00 in the morning and late into the night. And I just wanna tell you how much your dedication to our people and our causes has meant to me personally, and to our people. Really, you have dedicated your entire life to this, and so I wanna thank you so much, Ne Wah, for everything that you continue to do for us. And thank you for being here today.

**[27:33] Joseph J. Heath:** Thank you for those kind words. I often joke I don't know any better. What I really mean is I have had the privilege of learning from people, first the Attica Brothers and now the Haudenosaunee leaders and clan mothers. What a wonderful privilege to be able to understand the true meaning of justice. And we have a, a great, w- Here we are, where the Confederacy was formed, where the Great Law of Peace-

**[27:59] Betty Lyons Hill:** Mm-hmm.

**[27:59] Joseph J. Heath:** ... was formed. Let's live under it.

**[28:03] Betty Lyons Hill:** Yes. And it should matter to everyone. It's the birthplace of democracy, and this should be important to everyone. Thank you for listening. Thank you for coming today.
