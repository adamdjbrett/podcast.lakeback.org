# Episode 08: Joseph J. Heath on Land Rights and Onondaga Lake, Part 2

In this podcast episode, we explore the central theme and question of the podcast. How and why would anyone seek to pollute the sacred?

## Conversation

**[02:09] Betty Lyons Hill:** My original name is Kainhiah, which means small sky. My colonized name is Betty Lyons Hill, and I am a proud citizen of the Onondaga Nation Snipe Clan, and I am here welcoming today Joe Heath, who is general counsel for the Onondaga Nation. Welcome, Joe.

**[02:35] Joseph J. Heath:** Thank you. It's good to be here.

**[02:37] Betty Lyons Hill:** We are so grateful to have you today and your decades and decades of work and dedication to our nation, to our people. And now I wanna turn our attention a little bit to Onondaga Lake and the fight that we have had, that I've had the privilege of learning from you, and working with you and listening to you and watching you, try to help us gain a piece of our sacred lake back. If you could explain to the listeners, the history of that, the process, going back to, 2016, if you could touch on that.

**[03:07] Joseph J. Heath:** I think most people listening understand that Onondaga Lake is sacred to the Onondagas and the Haudenosaunee because it's here on the shores of this lake that the peacemaker brought the five warring nations together, had them bury their weapons of war and form the confederacy under the Great Law of Peace, and form the governments that are still in place at Onondaga and Tonawanda and Tuscarora, where the women play key roles and everybody is treated equally. And, the women choose the chiefs, and the chiefs can be removed, and all decisions are made by consensus. This should be a World Heritage Site given the importance of what happened here. Congress has recognized the importance of the confederacy to the structure of their constitution, and yet locally here, this current county government seems to treat the lake as a piece of real estate that they can exploit. But part of this support for the land rights action, which we talked about, resulted in, citizens, primarily, then businessman Lloyd Withers, approaching the county legislature and getting them to pass a resolution in 2011 to return a portion of land on the lake to the nation.

**[04:23] Joseph J. Heath:** There was some very good, appreciation of the importance of the lake to the nation built into the resolution. At that time, we were looking at a place called Murphy's Island, which is-- No one knows who Murphy is, and it's not an island, but that's, beside the point. And it looks very good. It's got some really great trees, and it's one of the places the eagles hang out in the winter. But it turns out as we took closer looks at it over the years, it's probably one of the most polluted places on the lake. But it was a very important step in 2011 for the legislature to do that. And that was under, when Nick Pirro was the executive, I believe. Then, we had a change and Joanie Mahoney took over. She asked the legislature to end that promise and to rescind that resolution. Rather than do that, the county legislature, under the leadership of then Chairperson Ryan McMahon, passed a new resolution. He actually got off his chair and went down into the main floor to speak in favor of this resolution that said, "We reaffirm the county's commitment to return land on the nation."

**[05:37] Joseph J. Heath:** I don't remember whether or not it actually identified, Maple Bay or not, but it was very clear that by then we'd done a lot of exploration and knew that Maple Bay was the only area that was clean enough for Onondaga's subsistence and cultural uses. Shortly after that was passed, you know, we toured Maple Bay with Mr. McMahon, and we showed him that there was an alternative trail because of this fixation the county has with their Tour of the Lake trail, and it seemed to be a very positive day. He made a promise to Tadodaho and you with a tear in his eye, "Oh, I have the political capital to do this, and I'm gonna do it." And so we thought the momentum was building. I began to have a series of meetings with the county attorney's office to deal with some of the issues that they say were related, and we were making progress on all of those. And then in 2022 was the last meeting that the county attorney would have with us.

**[06:39] Joseph J. Heath:** That's the last time that either the county attorney or the county executive has talked directly with my office. I don't think we've had any direct meetings with him since then either.

**[06:50] Betty Lyons Hill:** We have not.

**[06:51] Joseph J. Heath:** And so they've just stopped talking. And so that's where we are. We have another two broken promises. And that's why, you and others have done so much work, and it's time that the county stop making excuses and keep the promises, and return land on the lake to the nation.

**[07:10] Betty Lyons Hill:** I agree. I think, when I, look back on all these years of work trying to gain this piece back, and how much time has passed where we could have had a real relationship again, a healing relationship with the lake and all that inhabit her, all the living beings on that lake. I think that our listeners, some of the folks that live within this area sometimes get confused about their right to the recreational use of something, as though it's an inanimate object. They wanna have their Lake-the-Loop Trail. They wanna be able to bike on it, run on it, fish on it.

**[07:49] Betty Lyons Hill:** And those things are all, fine and great, to be able to have that kind of, interaction. But they're missing the overall that, all the living beings that inhabit that lake, are in a very toxic environment. The toxins have bioaccumulated in their systems, the plant life. A lot of medicinal plants that have gone away, because of that, contamination, which you fought so hard to try to ensure that the lake had a full cleanup. I'm wondering if you would talk a little bit about the work that you did, with, Honeywell about, when they said that they were gonna clean it up and those levels that they, had talked about, the different monetary levels of cleanup, and where that went.

**[08:32] Joseph J. Heath:** Most people that are listening probably have heard about the Superfund lawsuit that, was filed against Allied Chemical in the late '80s. Allied bought Honeywell in the '90s and kept the Honeywell name because it's more user-friendly. Between the two corporations, they're responsible for 140 Superfund sites around the country, more than any other corporation. And so Honeywell is a master at avoiding their responsibilities for their, pollution. We were not part of the Superfund. In 2005, when we saw, I remember the then regional director came to the longhouse to, we were beginning to get some traction to convince them that they needed to at least talk to us. And so he came to us and said, "Well, here's the remedy that we're gonna have Honeywell implement in terms of the lake bottom."

**[09:24] Joseph J. Heath:** The lake has 12 Superfund sites. That's just horrendous in itself. One of those is the lake bottom and the sediment itself, because mercury was dumped in that lake every day from 1946 to 1970, and so the sediment of this lake is horribly polluted. And what's important for people to understand is that 90 to 95% of the chemicals that were dumped in the land, in the lake, in the groundwater remain there, 95%. And so the bottom of the lake contains mercury and 26 other carcinogenic chemicals. And yet the dredging program that, we get bragged about so much did almost nothing to correct that. Proof of that happened last May when the county just down the shoreline here said, "Oh, we have to make the marina accommodate larger boats, so we're gonna do some testing."

**[10:19] Joseph J. Heath:** They claim to have been surprised that they found mercury in that sediment. I can tell you that our office and the nation was not a bit surprised, because what we said for the last 21 years since that remedy was, that farce was, aired is, "You're not cleaning it up. You're leaving everything in place, and then you're trying to contain it." You try to contain it with the cap. The cap has failed repeatedly. There's one section of the lake over near the factories, near Salve, near the visitor center, that has a mile-and-a-half long steel barrier wall. Because rather than clean the chemicals out of the ground and the groundwater that the factories dumped and then the groundwater carries them naturally into the lake, rather than clean it up, they try to contain it and then treat it.

**[11:08] Joseph J. Heath:** So you have to pump and treat the water continuously. You get very toxic materials out of that, and that system keeps failing. So I could go on and on. I have talks I give on the lake that last much longer than we have here. It's a very, very polluted lake. And unfortunately, when we tried to change the direction of that litigation, we-- the nation hired a, a dredging consultant, the same one the EPA used against GE on the Hudson River. Spent a half million dollars to come up with a proper dredging plan. And when then we tried to intervene in the lawsuit. The federal judge denied our motion almost before the ink was dry. That federal judge, by the way, just happens to be the same US attorney that wanted to get Dennis Banks back in the '80s, and but I'm sure that's unrelated.

**[12:01] Joseph J. Heath:** But the point really is the nation has never been able to have a real say about cleaning up this sacred lake. We have been ignored repeatedly, and the result is something that our children and grandchildren are gonna have to clean up. It's not clean. It's still poisoning people. The fish are poisoning people as we speak. And that's because the state, federal, and county government have been irresponsible in not forcing a proper cleanup, and in then trying to deny that the lake's polluted. Maybe the best example of that is the fact that the fish in the lake are highly toxic. The State Department of Health has a warning that says women and children should never eat any fish. They claim that men can eat one filet of one species a month. What we know from observations and from working with Catholic Charities is that refugees fish this lake every day they're eating like 40 meals a month out of this lake. And they're being poisoned-

**[13:07] Betty Lyons Hill:** Mm-hmm.

**[13:07] Joseph J. Heath:** because the county will not put up signs and will not actively prevent people from eating the fish, because doing that would be an admission that the lake isn't clean, and that's just something they're not willing to do.

**[13:21] Betty Lyons Hill:** No, they're not. And I think, that, it's egregious, and I think that they understand that. And by admitting that, and not preventing it, they've opened themselves up, in my opinion, to those folks, for litigation because it is criminal that they don't properly, let people know what's going on in that lake, folks that come from all over to settle here. And, we fought for 10 years just to have a proper fish sign put up, which only the city would put up. And the county put up a form of, that sign, their own sign with a happy fish jumping out of the water, and not really saying what, was contained in those fish.

**[14:00] Betty Lyons Hill:** And, as you recall, they had, someone that worked for the county that talked about, "Well, they're safe to eat as long as they're 15 inches or less." These folks that are coming here, they're eating these fish for subsistence. It's, they're doing so to be able to survive and to live. This is how they lived in their own home countries, and they come here to do the same thing. And so they're not measuring the fish, by any stretch of the imagination. And then, you think about the sewage problem that still remains today. Like, I'm surprised today... I don't know if you came down Onondaga Lake Parkway, if it was open or not, but you can smell the raw sewage that still is spilling into the lake on a daily basis.

**[14:39] Betty Lyons Hill:** It's so noxious. It comes into this building that we're in now.

**[14:44] Joseph J. Heath:** Because they're not monitoring and they're not dealing with, the failures in those systems. Every day it rains, and we certainly had one of those, raw sewage goes into the creeks and into the lake. It's called a combined sewer overflows. About 15 years ago, we worked with the county and Atlantic States to make a major improvement in that, but there are still dozens of places where the system is designed. When the volume of water gets too great, it's designed to open up valves and dump, raw untreated sewage into the creek and into the lakes. And it's just another example of the county being irresponsible and in denying the reality of what the problems are, and particularly human health problems.

**[15:32] Joseph J. Heath:** There are other examples of clear human health violations, and the county's very aware of them. And yet, whenever they talk about the lake now we hear the magic words cleanup because it, most days it doesn't stink the way it used to. We used to have a pipe that flow, and it came over 690, and you could see the chemicals going in the lake. So need to understand there have been some improvements. The problem is it's such a minor amount of what should happen, and yet this sacred lake remains a contained hazardous waste site.

**[16:05] Betty Lyons Hill:** I agree. And, the thought that they wanted to even put a swimmable beach, on the lake on the, eastern shore, and, we protested that as well. They did a celebrity swim, if you remember, with about 50, folks that they ended up having to take a boat out, I don't know, 100 feet off the shoreline for them to jump in because it was too toxic for them to walk in. I mean, that speaks volumes. And so for them to act surprised that where these boats are being kept and held and docked, that those sites would have extensive mercury is, you know, mind-blowing to me. It's as though that they can shut it off, and only address and think about what they wanna deal with, on any given day instead of looking at the total picture of what's happening to the lake and all that inhabit her.

**[16:58] Joseph J. Heath:** About a week ago, I was asked to speak in an urban ecology class up at the forestry school about this. And in the process of that, we began to talk about how much better life would be for the people who live in the city of Syracuse if this lake were really clean and they could take advantage of that, perhaps a small percentage of what the decision-makers take advantage of when they live out on their mansions on Skaneateles Lake and Cazenovia Lake.

**[17:28] Betty Lyons Hill:** Right.

**[17:29] Joseph J. Heath:** This city has the worst child poverty in the country, and we're denying them the right to be able to recreate and use a clean lake because it's just, leaders don't care. And they won't make the right decisions, and they won't even talk to us.

**[17:47] Betty Lyons Hill:** That's right. You know, Tadodaho Sid Hill, we interviewed him yesterday. He did our, podcast yesterday, and, he talked about, that politicians, rarely ever do the right thing, but that people can. And so my question, my last question to you is what can people do to try to help, in these efforts, not only to get the lake clean, but to see the nation and the Haudenosaunee Confederacy itself gain a piece, of the lake back?

**[18:13] Joseph J. Heath:** Well, they can continue to support the nation and come to events and, understand the importance that the nation's efforts around the lake mean to everybody here. As I just said, if this lake were clean, the advantages and the improvements in the quality of life for people in the city of Syracuse would be tremendous. And so we hope that people can continue to, do what they did last November, which was change the composition of the county legislature into a much more progressive, responsible legislative body that cares about all of the citizens of, Onondaga County, and continue to hold the county's feet to the fire to make them keep their promises, and to also continue to put the pressure on New York State to make amends for all this land they've stolen.

**[19:08] Joseph J. Heath:** What we found when we were able to get 1,000 acres returned, which includes the headwaters of the creek, was that when the nation works with enough partners in a positive way, consistent with that first paragraph of the Land Rights Action, we get much better results. And so we ask people to continue to support the nation's rights, to understand the history, to go to the Great Law of Peace Center just down the road here, and listen to the Onondaga elders so that they understand the importance of Onondaga Lake culturally, spiritually, symbolically, historically to all of us, and to remember that the whole world is watching. Onondaga County is, i- is understood by people outside as not being responsible. When you won't even talk to another government, that's the other thing we haven't even mentioned here. The Supreme Court just ruled six years ago that we're sitting inside a federal reservation right here.

**[20:15] Joseph J. Heath:** One mile around this lake is a federal reservation, and yet nothing has changed in the county's position. They continue to make money off it and develop it in their definition, and the nation gets no benefit from that. We need people to continue to support us and hold the county responsible to do the right thing because it's time for justice. It's time for healing. And citizens can push, the county executive and the county legislature to support the nation and the return of land on this lake. We could have a very wonderful ceremonial event. It would bring people together, and that's what we will have sooner or later. And we just have to keep working towards that.
