# Episode 06: Tadodaho Sidney Hill on the Peacemaker and Onondaga Lake

## Introduction

**[00:00] Adam DJ Brett:** When Tadodaho Sidney Hill was a boy, he danced at the New York State Fair beside Onondaga Lake. What he remembers most about that experience was the smell. That was how an Onondaga child knew the lake then. No one fished it. Nobody swam in it. It lived on in the stories: the lake that the Peacemaker crossed, carrying the Great Law of Peace. The shore where a powerful man named Tadodaho was the last holdout and was finally won over, and the five nations became one as the Haudenosaunee Confederacy. His mother would tell him stories about an elder who lived across the road, a giant of a man who had survived the Carlisle Indian School and often spoke in the longhouse. He used to walk around the lake alone, caring for the lake. He never told anyone what he did there.

**[00:57] Adam DJ Brett:** When he was a little boy, Sidney Hill was given the name Haihodagos, or "he brings up the subjects." Names, he says, have a way of fitting into what one does later in life. Born into the Onondaga Nation Eel Clan, after his uncle passed, he was asked to take up the title that began on that shore. For thirty years, he has been the Tadodaho of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy, and he is still bringing up the subject.

**[01:26] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** Winter, summer, fall, spring, it was there. So our people were never- were never hungry.

*[01:33] Theme music. [Chris Thomas singing in Onondaga]*

**[01:36] Adam DJ Brett:** You are listening to Healing the Sacred, a podcast from the American Indian Law Alliance about restoring Onondaga Lake through story, ceremony, and public scholarship. The producers of this podcast would like to acknowledge with respect the Onondaga Nation, Firekeepers of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy, the Indigenous peoples whose ancestral and unceded lands include Onondaga Lake. Since the inception of the United Nations Permanent Forum on Indigenous Issues, Tadodaho Sidney Hill has opened the weeks-long forum with the Thanksgiving Address. And now, introducing your host, Betty Lyons Hill, a proud citizen of the Onondaga Nation Snipe Clan. She is the Executive Director of the American Indian Law Alliance.

## Conversation

**[02:35] Betty Lyons Hill:** My original name is Gaeñ hia, my colonized name is Betty Lyons Hill, and I am so privileged and excited to have our guest today, our Tadodaho Sid Hill, who happens to be my husband. I would like for you to introduce yourself.

**[02:54] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** Yes, my name is Sidney Hill. My given- my name as a child was Haihodagos, which means he brings up the subject. And some of their names kind of almost like, are fitted for you, what you do. Later in life, I was asked to take the position of Tadodaho after my uncle passed. I assume that- I assume that position.

**[03:28] Betty Lyons Hill:** 1997.

**[03:29] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** 30 years. I've been there for 30 years.

**[03:33] Betty Lyons Hill:** What I would like you to start off is, to tell our listeners, those that would have no idea about our Confederacy or your title. Give a little background history of your title and what it represents and how that came to be.

**[03:50] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** Well, it's quite a- quite an epic story. When some of our elders come and recite it, I think when one elder came one time and he said, I- I think he did it in seven days, but he said, "We usually do this in 10 days." It takes 10 days where people can really get into the details of the story. Now we do a- a much shorter version of it, trying to translate to our people. And having to translate to people is the effects of the boarding schools. But the story of- of the Peacemaker, a lot of times we- we start out with three messages, three great messages that we went by as a people, you know, of Haudenosaunee. The first is when we were given the ceremonies, and that's- that's quite a tale in its- in itself. Where we're given the ceremonies and they said we start getting away from this, and it soon- it soon happened that the- our people weren't safe. There was a lot of fighting. There was cannibalism.

**[05:07] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** Women, children, elders were not safe anymore. So they- they, one of the families, went- had to hide, kind of hide. This one woman was hiding her daughter, hoping that they wouldn't be killed or- or basically eaten from, you know, from the cannibalism that was going on. And nobody was safe. And they went and isolated themselves. And that- that part there was- was so, you know, detailed, where all of a sudden the young girl became- became old enough to bear children.

**[05:52] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** And by being isolated in- in the woods, in the forest, she never saw a man. So her- her grandmother drilled her and was starting to get angry with her because she wouldn't say, and the- the girl didn't know what happened. She didn't know. She didn't know no man. And subsequently they- they had a- had a little boy. And the grandmother, being ashamed, not wanting to tell the people that what had happened to her- to her daughter, was, made three attempts to kill- kill the- the baby, and, but was unsuccessful.

**[06:44] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** And one night she got a message to- that this- this boy was special, and he had a special duty to do, that she should support him in anything he does, and he's going to grow fast, and he's going to do some amazing things, but you need to support him. So, so that, and this next thing goes into this detail of how he grew up and what he was doing, and- and he wanted- he wanted to see the people. And that's when they start- they start talking about being peaceful, that everybody's related, that we shouldn't be arguing, we shouldn't be even with the children to be arguing and hitting each other, because we're all related. We're all cousins, and nobody's heard this before. The people were very astounded by this little boy and the way he was talking.

**[07:50] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** Okay, then you just- you just kind of fast-forward to when he was- when he was grown, and he wanted- wanted to set out to- to deliver his message, and he wanted to go to the east. And they warned him that this was the worst place to go because it was the land of the- the Mohawk, and they were very... And he said, "This is exactly where I want to go." And so he- so when he got there and he approached them, told them his message, but he had to prove himself. So they- so they gave him a test, and they asked him to climb this tree and- and, edge of this cliff, and that they would cut the tree down, and it would go into this gorge. And if he survived that, then they- they would believe and accept his message.

**[08:49] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** So they did this, climbed the tree, and the men chopped the tree down, and he fell into the gorge. They looked there for a while and didn't see any- any motion or any life down below and went back to the village. The next day, they seen this little fire with smoke coming out and some of the young men went over there to see what- what it was, and it was this man had a little fire going. And he said they would take him to their leader back into the village, and that's how they accepted- they accepted his message.

**[09:33] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** So he went... This is going to the Mohawks, and the next one was the Oneidas, and then the Onondagas, the Cayugas, and then the Senecas, which were the original five- five nations with the- the Great Law of Peace, the Confederacy. So, but as he was going through, as he went to the Oneidas and come to the Onondagas, and there was a great powerful man there who didn't really need this message and was powerful enough that didn't really accept it. So they had to skip the Onondagas and go on to the Cayugas and then the Senecas and come back.

**[10:19] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** And this man, this evil person was, Tadodaho, that was his name. And he, they said they would have to come back and as a group and approach him and see if he would accept this message. And so they- they went, got the few leaders and came back, and approached him. And through songs and determination, he, they were able to convince him to accept this message, and he accepted it. But they said, "Well, we'll give you- give you the Onondagas. We would be like the main- the main village of this confederacy." So anytime there was some problems or- or danger that come to any of the- the nations, they would come to Onondaga and try to see what they could do. And Onondaga would be like the capital of the Confederacy, and that Tadodaho would be his duty to call them people together.

**[11:29] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** And so they accepted that message, and it was, it's- it's been that- been that way since, I don't know how many thousand years now. So, you know, even today we still bring our people together at Onondaga. Certain- certain issues come, and a lot of- a lot of the issues right now are the border issues, and, you know, the hunting and fishing, all these- these issues that would bring danger to our people. And we bring those to Onondaga and do the best we can to try to see what our people can do to- to help our people.

**[12:14] Betty Lyons Hill:** So after he accepted the message of peace and took on that role, can you explain a little bit to our listeners how it came to be that the Peacemaker came to Onondaga Lake to bring that message?

**[12:34] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** Well, that's what- that's where they gathered, when they came back and, when they were trying to convince the Tadodaho to accept this message. It was right here on Onondaga Lake. There were- the village was on one side, and he lived on the other side and approached him across the- across the lake. Like I said, he- he did everything he could to stop them from- from getting to him, but they persevered. So this Onondaga Lake means sacred to us because all this was finalized. Our confederacy was here as soon as the Tadodaho accepted, and it was put together. And they were given- given all their duties as leaders and established the great law, Great Law of Peace, what we call the Gayaneshkaho. And that's been- been there. So, so this lake is like, I don't know, it's- it's- it's the beginning of, I think, real democracy to our people.

**[13:51] Betty Lyons Hill:** And then in other episodes that we've done, podcasts that we've done, we've talked about how the United States patterned their government after our government, leaving out the roles of women, the roles of rights and roles in the natural world.

**[14:05] Betty Lyons Hill:** What I don't think that we've mentioned is that Congress, both houses of Congress passed a Resolution 331 October the 4th in 1988 that basically said that they patterned their government after ours. And so, as you're looking at the lake now and you're seeing all of the contamination and the pollution, that it's still a Superfund site after over $500 million worth of remediation, how old do you think you were before you realized, in your memory, when people in your family were talking about the importance of that lake? Now that it's your responsibility, in that role as our Tadodaho, can you think back as to what family members that you had that maybe spoke to that?

**[14:56] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** Well, one thing I remember is my- my mother. We lived across the road from one of the elders there. This was a man who went to Carlisle, was a speaker in a longhouse. He was a giant of a man. He was like, he must've been like 6'6", 6'7", great athlete. And she said he would go to the Onondaga Lake and talk, probably offer tobacco, do something, but he never told anybody. She didn't know. And he would go to the lake, walk there, back then, and do whatever he did, he- he did not pass this on, or nobody continued it.

**[15:39] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** So that's, that was the earliest of, my- my mentioning of the lake, you know. And then, you know, going as a kid, going to the state fair and, sometimes we would, I would be one of the dancers at the state fair as a young boy. And going there, you could smell the lake. That's one of the biggest things that we, you know, we heard about the lake. So we were so disconnected from the lake. We only heard it in the stories of our- of the Peacemaker that crossed on that lake. But nobody used it. It was- it was contaminated. People didn't fish there. We didn't use it. And knew that it was just so contaminated that you couldn't even, wouldn't even go in the water.

**[16:36] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** So that- that's about all I knew about the lake until, you know, until we- until this man sued- tried to sue the, whoever to clean up the lake. And that was a catastrophe too, because they were given like five- five levels of- of cleanup. And, of course, it was like 2.3 billion to do a really good job down to 500 million, which was the lowest, and they chose the 500 million. And I don't think, even in my- in my kids' lifetime, they'll ever see a clean lake.

**[17:21] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** So there it sits, and we hear, "Oh, the lake is clean." You still don't see anybody swimming on the shores, or I see people fishing out there, but I don't, you know, we hope they're not eating them. And I guess they are. Some of the- some of the people who come to visit and come from other countries are used to fishing as part of their food source and go out there and fish and eat, eat the- eat the fish. And they've been said to have high levels of mercury.

**[18:00] Betty Lyons Hill:** We did a Department of Health study on the folks that are fishing, the folks that are coming into Syracuse as what they call, like, migrant population. I really don't like those terms. These are human beings, and they were eating about 40 meals a month out of this lake. I'm wondering, your thoughts on how this affects all of us as Haudenosaunee people, including the children, to know that our sacred lake is still a Superfund site, and it's still so polluted. Do you have any thoughts about how to help them with that?

**[18:40] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** Well, I- I think we can just look at the climate, the worries about the people. We've had another message about all the things that were going to come here, and we were told that one day we wouldn't be able to drink the water. We wouldn't be able to use the water. And back when I was a kid, I thought, "Well, it's not- it's not gonna happen now." But here we are buying water now. A lot of people having contamination issues. Lead in their water, mercury in their water, all kinds of things, and now it's affecting everybody with all the contamination. But it was just, never thought it- it'd come to this- to this level, where you- where you couldn't drink the water. You had to buy water now. And even that's, you buy it, most of the time you buy it out of a plastic bottle, there's- there's dangers in that too.

**[19:43] Betty Lyons Hill:** I think it's sad, when you think about our relationship to the lake and how, you know, we've been displaced from our proper relationship and proper place at the lake and all the living beings that inhabit her. I wanted to ask you a little bit about the fight to get the lake back and the land rights action that was filed in 2005. If you could explain the purpose and intention, 'cause I remember that when you were asked personally about it, you weren't talking about the displacement of people. You were trying to be very, very clear about that. Wondering if you could just tell our listeners a little bit about that.

**[20:24] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** Yeah, that's- that's- I always talked about that. I always heard the leaders talking about land- land rights actions and, we tried to not say [land claim](https://www.onondaganation.org/land-rights/). And the elders said, "No, we know what it's- what it's like to lose, to have to move," and our people have been moved as far as Wisconsin, Oklahoma, up into Canada. And so we had no intention of moving people off- off of their- their land. But still at the same time, what was done to us, losing our land.

**[21:05] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** And I just- when I think about the lake, and I think of all the knowledge that was there to- to use that lake, all the language, all the names of the fishes, all the names of the- the medicines and the food and the- the bird life, the animal life, all the science that went along with that lake, we have lost that, we've lost that. There's no way to get it back. It's gone. And something that would be hard to rejuvenate again because we still can't use the lake. So, the biggest- biggest loss besides not being able to use the lake and all the knowledge that was- that goes along with- with having that lake right in the middle of your community.

**[22:02] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** Our diet was, our one-third of our diet was fish. Winter, summer, fall, spring, it was there. So our people were never- were never hungry. This- this area is 25% of the world's fresh water and here we are right in the middle of it, the Finger Lakes, the Great Lakes, and we were just- we were just rich, wonderful. It was the best land to- to grow things you could ask for.

**[22:40] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** And so now you look at Onondaga Lake and Lake Erie, Lake Ontario, contaminated, you know, people just using it as a- as a sewage to get rid of their waste. So I wonder, you know, we're- we're supposed to be such intelligent people. Why would we not know that if you do this, you're not gonna be able to drink that water? And the water's life. So it's just, I just wonder sometimes, in the pursuit of money and financial gain, it's- it's, I think it blinds the- blinds some people's vision as to what's best for people.

**[23:33] Betty Lyons Hill:** It does. It absolutely does. I think about all the animals and the medicine plants that used to exist before that no longer exist, and how even the contamination still continues today and still exists, and how it is bioaccumulated into their beings, right?

**[23:53] Betty Lyons Hill:** As we continue to fight to try to get a piece of the lake back for our people, it's not just about recreational use. I think that people now see this lake as a commodity, yet still some sense of resource instead of it being about this relationship that we need to have with all the living beings that inhabit her to try to help heal her. In speaking to that, I was thinking about how sometimes you will go to the lake and you do our Ganohé:nio, the Thanksgiving Address, the words that come before all else. And I watch the animal life that reacts to those words, and wondered if you could speak to that, because our language is an earth language and it responds to that. And what do you hope to see by gaining a piece of the lake back?

**[24:50] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** I- I think it's just a- a reconnection with the lake. And, you know, if- I always thought if they had- they had cleaned up the lake with the 2.3 million- billion, that the lake would clean the rest of itself. It would probably have- have cleaned up 90% of the lake, and I think the lake could have survived and cleansed itself from there, and we- we would have been able to use the lake, and I think that's what we still hope for. We can't give up hope. We don't give up on this lake. Maybe some- maybe some people will- will understand this someday, it can be done. We should never give up on trying to heal this lake and know what a important part of this was to our people and the history of our people, the Confederacy.

**[25:57] Betty Lyons Hill:** I wonder if you could talk a little bit about the fight that we've had in trying to get the county executive to give a piece of the lake back, if you're willing or wanting to talk about it, and why does it seem that it's so hard to do the right thing?

**[26:17] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** Yeah, I always- I always think about that, you know, doing the right thing. It's politics. It's history. What it is we're- we're at. We're looking at the- at the map, and I said, this is just a little dot on that map. And can't see what a- what an issue it is of returning that little bit back to its original people just to have presence on there. But sometimes it's- it's hard for governments to do the right thing. I talked to our- our attorney, Joe Heath said one time, and he says, "There's doing the right thing and- and the law, and when the two come together, you have justice." And I always remembered that, what he said, you know. And I believe sometimes government politics. The government can't do the right thing, but the people can. There's enough- there's enough good people out there.

**[27:33] Betty Lyons Hill:** You can see that this, as it does most of us, affect you in a very profound personal way. I think about a time that our eldest son, Garrett, Oneganal, his name means cool water. We were driving on the west shore of the lake. We were talking with him about that and that it wasn't clean and- and that they were all gonna clean up part of the lake, and it still might not be safe. And so his very basic question was, "Did they ask the fish? The fish?" And it kind of really touched us about, and- in our teachings, we're supposed to listen to children when they speak, because they can see clear things. They're not, they're- they're not contaminated. They're pure. And they only see truth. So when they- when they ask a question or- or- or question you, then you're supposed to listen, and because that- that is coming from a pure person.

**[28:48] Betty Lyons Hill:** So the last thing that I wanted to ask you is, you know, when you're thinking about these kids and you're talking about trying to give them hope, right? What do you see for the future of our children, Haudenosaunee children, all Haudenosaunee children, not just Onondagas, but all Haudenosaunee children, 'cause this lake is so important and sacred to us. If in an ideal world, what would you like to see?

**[29:18] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** Well, I think without, you know, it's- there is ways to clean this lake, and our children see what- what their ancestors, their grandparents and parents have done or- or not done, you know. But the- there is the capability. You know, we have the technology, and there's things that you- we do is unbelievable today, but yet we can't clean up this one small lake, you know, so maybe- maybe some man with- with some power can change the priorities of our people and put them in the right direction.

**[30:04] Betty Lyons Hill:** Thank you so much for joining us today. I appreciate your time, and we can certainly keep encouraging and leading by example, and appreciate your example of continuing to fight for a clean lake and for a piece of it back. So thank you so much for joining us.

**[30:25] Tadodaho Sidney Hill:** Thank you.
