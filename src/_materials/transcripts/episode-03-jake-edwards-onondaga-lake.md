# Episode 03: Jake Edwards on Onondaga Lake, the Thanksgiving Address, and Land Rights

## Introduction

**[00:00] Adam DJ Brett:** In the late 1960s, Jake Edwards watched a pipe pour yellow-green sludge into Onondaga Lake. Can you imagine what it must feel like to see the lake you love, the lake that is foundational to your nation and your people, being polluted? Fresh water and animal habitats replaced with yellow-green sludge.

**[00:21] Jake Edwards:** I could not be a environmentalist because I am the environment. And he said, "And what you're doing to the waters, what you're doing to the earth, you're doing to all the people."

*[00:33] Theme music. [Chris Thomas singing in Onondaga]*

**[00:36] Adam DJ Brett:** You are listening to Healing the Sacred, a podcast from the American Indian Law Alliance about restoring Onondaga Lake through story, ceremony, and public scholarship. The producers of this podcast would like to acknowledge with respect the Onondaga Nation, Firekeepers of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy, the Indigenous peoples whose ancestral and unceded lands include Onondaga Lake. And now, introducing your host, Betty Lyons Hill, a proud citizen of the Onondaga Nation Snipe Clan. She is the Executive Director of the American Indian Law Alliance.

## Conversation

**[01:13] Betty Lyons Hill:** Welcome. Thank you so much for joining us today. My name is Betty Lyons Hill. I am a citizen of the Onondaga Nation Snipe Clan and executive director of the American Indian Law Alliance. Today, I am so grateful to be able to introduce to all of you one of my mentors and close dear friend, Jake Edwards of the Eel Clan. Welcome, Jake.

**[01:35] Jake Edwards:** Haiwhagai’i I'm from the Eel Clan of the Onondaga Nation.

**[01:44] Betty Lyons Hill:** Thank you so much for joining us today and making time for this incredibly important conversation. So I wanna start off first because I've known you so long, but I never had the opportunity to be able to ask you such in-depth questions about your relationship with our sacred lake, Onondaga Lake. So my first question is, when you think about Onondaga Lake, what does that mean to you, and why is understanding the story so vital for everyone to hear?

**[02:20] Jake Edwards:** When we were younger, we used to hear about the lake, but we never had transportation to come down and visit it. And when we did back in the '60s, 19... late '60s, one of the first things we noticed was a trolley of some sort overhead, and it was pumping yellow-greenish stuff into the lake over near the state fairgrounds. And so as far as enjoying the waters of the lake, we never had that opportunity. First of all, we couldn't get here, and then when we did finally come to the lake to look at what was told to us of the years gone by that had the beginning of our confederacy, the unity of our nations right here on the shores of the lake, we couldn't even get to that.

**[03:07] Jake Edwards:** Otherwise, it was private lands and posted, and there was a little park that we could go to. And we were told that our elders used to come down and have ceremony for what was at the beginning of our unity of Haudenosaunee, that they would have a feast, and we'd conduct that feast there. Although I wasn't there, it was long before my time, they used to come down here, and then they got banned from coming down here to conduct their pagan, what they called pagan, rituals, because they had tourist attractions here at the lake. And so we were banned. Onondaga was banned from doing that. So they had to sneak around the other side of the lake and still conduct their thanksgiving and feast, recognizing the ancestors' work to contributing to the unity of the Haudenosaunee and the founding of democracy.

**[04:00] Betty Lyons Hill:** When you were growing up, you had a lot of people that influenced and taught you about the history of the lake, and I was hoping that you would talk a little bit about your uncle in particular, as I know that you are so close, and he imparted such wisdom in you. Wanted to just ask you, what did he use to say about the lake?

**[04:21] Jake Edwards:** Well, he was upset about it becoming a dumping grounds, because he used to tell the stories of all the whitefish that was gathered here, and they would spawn and go up the stream, up Onondaga Creek right to our village. And they used to talk about that as being so abundant in fish that you could walk across their backs. The waters were just full of fish spawning. And then that started disappearing the more that the colonizers were moving into our lands, the fish were disappearing. And we found out later that they were netting them and capturing them and sending them off to New York City, and they're in boatloads. So the fish population finally disappeared as far as the whitefish are concerned.

**[05:01] Jake Edwards:** And then the contamination that started coming into the waters, and that's when I first noticed it as a young lad, that pipe that went across the highway, and it was pouring in, and I questioned about it. I questioned my mom and uncle. They were both in a car. We were going to the Indian village at the fair. And Uncle was just upset about it because of what they're doing to the water, and he didn't-- He used to work at Crucible Steel, and he said he knows what was being put in the trenches of the floors, and that was being pumped directly into the lake. And my mom said that they know that they're contaminating the water, and when a complaint was made, they would stop, pay the fine, and start pumping again. They had fines assessed to them, and they would just pay the fine and continue dumping. And so that's situation that added to the contamination of the lake bottom here at Onondaga [lake], on the south shore where it's the most contaminated is right exactly where that pipe was pouring right in.

**[05:59] Jake Edwards:** We saw it pouring right in, right off. It was about 20 feet above the water or more, and it was just pouring down yellow sludge, yellow-orangish-green sludge. It was scary to see because you don't see that in nature anywheres you go, them kind of colors. And that's what we know of as contaminating the lake was coming right from the industry right there, which was Allied Chemical and, which is Honeywell and Arm & Hammer. I forget their name, Dwight, White, something like that. Some Blue Circle was another one. General Electric. We start looking into that because of the color of it is what got us looking into the environmental damage, and we talked with some people from Syracuse University who came out to visit my uncle, and he had some pretty good one-liners to their questions, and they said they invited him to a podium speech for the students on environmentalist.

**[07:07] Jake Edwards:** He said they told him that he'd make a very good environmentalist, and these students need to hear you. And he said, "I couldn't become a environmentalist." And they questioned him like, "Well, why can't you? You got all the answers that fit right into the topics here." He said, "I could not be a environmentalist because I am the environment." And he said, "And what you're doing to the waters, what you're doing to the earth, you're doing to all the people." So he never did go to SU to speak.

**[07:38] Betty Lyons Hill:** That's a beautiful way to put it, because I myself don't like the word environmentalist or activist. I think those are words that we don't, we don't associate well with. So thank you for putting it that way, because it's so true. We are the environment. He had a depth of knowledge that was just beyond most people in his thinking, the way he thought and how he articulated it, and I think that you've definitely picked that up. So my next question is about the full extent of that contamination. When did you fully realize how bad it really was? You saw it as a child, and you saw these terrible colors of sludge and contamination going into the lake. When did you realize how bad it really was, right? The depth of that contamination?

**[08:24] Jake Edwards:** Well, they have this museum here, the Salt Museum, and we were supposed to take a trip to go look at it in our younger years, and it wasn't open yet. So we walked to the lake, and there was a whole bunch of fish floating belly up. It was startling really, had a terrible stench to it. And so we questioned one of the park workers that we came across and asked him what's going on with the fish. And he said, "Oh, it happens with the change of temperatures." And that didn't fit right 'cause we fished in all temperatures. And all seasons we did fishing, and we have never seen that happen. I've never seen that happen. And so when we finally got to the museum, they said that they pawned it off as far as a fish kill caused by the temperature of the water.

**[09:16] Jake Edwards:** And to me, that was just another story to cover up something either they didn't know about or they caused. That's the feeling I got. And so I started really paying attention to the contamination around the streams. And so we paddled, we got in some canoes, and we paddled the waters to look and see what is spilling into the creek, and we've been following that ever since. And that was back in the '80s, early '80s. And we're finding it still going on today. They revamped the overflow system of Syracuse, flood and sewage, and I don't know how many billions they put into it, but they said they fixed that. It was the following year, the following paddling season that we went through, and it was pouring right out right from where they said they fixed it. And we couldn't take pictures because it was raining and we were in canoes. And so we sent somebody after we got to shore to take pictures. So there's pictures out there, but they did admit to it that it is an overflow situation of the storms, storm drains in Syracuse that they can't keep up with.

**[10:18] Betty Lyons Hill:** I actually believe that it's more than that, because on any given day, you can drive down Onondaga Lake Parkway without any storm and smell the raw sewage pouring in from the east side into the lake. So I know that's something that we'll have to visit later on. But after $500 million worth of remediation to this lake, it's still a Superfund site. And I kinda wanted to hear about your own personal feelings about... I know how it affects me on such a personal level, like when we go to do our Thanksgiving address, there's a contamination that still lies on the bottom of this lake in the waters and that's bioaccumulated into the plants. And that connection with the Thanksgiving address and that contamination, I would like you to talk a little bit about why we do that Thanksgiving address and why we continue to go there to do that so that, for all the living beings that live on and around the lake.

**[11:14] Jake Edwards:** As far as the Thanksgiving address, the Ganohé:nio’, there’s no room for contamination in there. And so we always share that, whether we're at hardships throughout our times, the invasion of our lands from the colonizers, we still carry it on 'cause we're still talking to our relatives that we're all related, whether it's the trees or the four-leggeds or the winged animals, we still have gratitude that they're still here. And they're going through what we're going through also, as far as the contaminations and the invasions that occur in our lands here. And so we continue to do the gratitude because our elders always told us that we have to carry on the original instructions of thanksgiving to our mother and all that was put here, because everything that we need to live and survive in a peaceful way is here for us.

**[12:07] Jake Edwards:** And so there we share our gratitude with all that's here for us. And some of it may be weak, like the fish in the waters, we feel that they'll come back, but we still have to push the colonizers to properly clean up the lake and steer their waste in different manners of cleaning it up properly. So we have to continue to push them.

**[12:31] Betty Lyons Hill:** I think about Sarah Shute and Tina Thomas and how they were playing the Ganohé:nio, the Thanksgiving Address, over at the Skä•noñh Great Law of Peace Center, and after over 200 years, the eagles decided to nest there, and I don't believe that's an accident, because our language is an earth language. It's one born of the earth. And I was wondering, the Great Law of Peace, how does the Great Law of Peace inform the way we ought to view our responsibilities to one another in this land? I wondered if you could just let our listeners know a little bit about that.

**[13:04] Jake Edwards:** Well, the Great Law of Peace was set down to unite our minds in peace and contentment, and the decisions that we make are to no way negatively affect seven generations coming. So when we gather, we do our hunting and gathering and planting throughout our lands, throughout our house here in the Eastern Woodlands, that we always share the gratitude of what was put here. And the great law of peace unites us and helps us understand that humans have different feelings. And there is mechanisms put in place to deal with those different feelings and different ideas so that we can discuss it and share our different ideas and come of one mind for the betterment of all of our people's feelings.

**[13:48] Jake Edwards:** We acknowledge all the humans and all the beings.

**[13:50] Betty Lyons Hill:** I like that. Talking about the lake, you once said that Christians should put as much into healing as they put into the destruction and into what they've caused. What would that healing look like?

**[14:02] Jake Edwards:** They'd have to back up to their own archives and their own policies of what their intentions were when they came across to this land. It wasn't about the human beings walking about on the land or the animal beings walking about. It was about the land itself and what they can get from it, not understanding what was put here on Earth was put here for all of us to be at peace and contentment here on Mother Earth as we walk about. So when I talk about the efforts that the Christian people should be establishing is understanding all the work that they put in to disrupt our people, to disrupt us in our communities, in our cultures. It's a form of genocide that still goes on today. And the influence that these people had put here and the revenues that they obtained from the lands of our peoples should be reversed in putting us in a comfortable place where we can carry on our cultures and reverse the genocide.

**[15:03] Betty Lyons Hill:** I think about the Jesuits and when they first came here and how our people ran them off. And yet, unfortunately, there's still a very large segment of the population in and around the Syracuse area that likes to tell this false narrative of the lake and its ownership. My question is, 'cause I can't seem to come to this answer myself other than the fact that it behooves them to keep telling this false narrative. Why do they cling to this story? Why don't they wanna hear the real story and the true story? There's so much power in it that I think it would be meaningful to all citizens of the United States to understand that this, as you said earlier, the birthplace of democracy. Why do they continue to do that? So it's kind of a philosophical question for you to answer.

**[15:52] Jake Edwards:** Like they say, the truth hurts. And then to get into the true meaning of their missions was to come and take our lands and destroy us as a people, subdue us, destroy us, and all of what their doctrine of discovery tells them they can do about taking land that's inhabited by non-Christians. And so they're afraid to face the truth because the truth hurts so much, and they've been telling their children and grandchildren how good it is to be a Christian without sharing the paths of what got them to this land here and what their mission really was. The policies of New York State coming into New York State times is the negotiating and the discussions to obtain our lands.

**[16:46] Jake Edwards:** The records of those talks and policies were destroyed in the fire of 1911 in Albany, conveniently destroyed the documents stating their policy of how to obtain our land. And that was through the aspects of coercion, bribery, thievery, and the false representation of leadership on the signature pages of these New York State quasi-treaties. And we call them quasi-treaties not because they're fake and almost real. We call them that because that's what the United States calls them. They helped fund New York State's quasi-treaties with some federal money. Instead of taking it back as New York State violated federal law, they just rode with them because they have the same policy of removal of indigenous people off the lands.

**[17:39] Jake Edwards:** And so they just allowed New York State to carry on their thievery of our lands. And most of these decisions that were being made on the policy of our removal of our people and the taking of our land took place in churches throughout Albany, throughout Orange, Fort Orange, and around that area, Schenectady. And a lot of the places that these discussions were being held were in gin mills, saloons, and churches on how to obtain our lands.

**[18:12] Betty Lyons Hill:** It's always a struggle, too, when I think about the lake itself after they've taken possession, if you will, of the lake and its area, and how sacred it is to us. And yet all they can see, I feel, is the recreational use of this lake. They don't see it as a living being and all of the living beings on it. They wanna use it to run around or to walk around or to bike. They have a bike path. They wanna be able to boat on it. And it brings me back to our own mandate as Onkwehonwe people that we are supposed to make sure that we care for this land and protect it for the seventh generation unborn. So I was wondering if you could talk a little bit about that because that's this piece that there's a disconnect. How do we have non-native people see the value in that connection? That they don't see it as inanimate objects but that they're our relatives.

**[19:07] Jake Edwards:** If you look at the Doctrine of Discovery and what that states about, they have rights to this. They have rights to the land, to the waters, and to the people here. That's all false because the water has its own rights. And when you talk about the paths and the tourism coming to look at Onondaga Lake but don't touch the water, that's also a false because one of the ways to help any life threatened by contamination or anything like that is to let it be. Take out what you put in there and let it be, let it recover. Don't crowd it out with what caused it in the first place, was tourism to the lake. And so these paths here are just bringing in the public to enjoy what's been contaminated and not give it enough room to breathe on its own to heal.

**[20:00] Betty Lyons Hill:** The struggle that we've faced in trying to just gain a piece of our sacred lake back so that we could do exactly that, just let it heal. I wanted you to talk a little bit about what that meant to you, means to you personally. I know what it means to me, to have this fight, been in this fight for so long, in trying to get a piece of the lake back. Why would it be beneficial for us to have that interaction?

**[20:21] Jake Edwards:** Because we speak for the animals, we speak for the trees, and we speak for the waters as they do for us within ceremony and our cultural ways of life and the balance that we all should carry as we walk about here. So back in 1922,[¹](#everett-note){#everett-ref role=doc-noteref aria-label="Footnote 1"} Albany, I asked why we can't get to the lake anymore to conduct our ceremony and our Thanksgiving and our feast for our ancestors and what they accomplished here on the shores of Onondaga Lake. And so throughout the years, New York State's been trying to rid us, get rid of us and move us, destroy us, and so forth. And so they commissioned this fellow by the name of Edward Everett to do a report on New York State's Indian problem, and trying to find a way to legally, in their legal eyes, which is way out of balance of nature, to get rid of us.

**[21:22] Jake Edwards:** And Edward Everett's report showed the opposite, said that the Haudenosaunee have rights to this land and New York State violated their own laws and federal laws. So New York State didn't accept Edward Everett's report, and it was dormant for about 50 years. The report wasn't read by anybody, and somebody found it. It reappeared in 1970, 1971, and it was brought to our leaders that you guys should read this and understand this, that the facts of the land transactions were fraudulent. The facts that New York State has jurisdiction over this land is fraudulent. And so that's what started off with my generation of understanding the wrongs that they've done to obtain our lands, and it's becoming very clear that what our elders were telling us is the same thing as what they've been documenting.

**[22:22] Jake Edwards:** But their documents somehow burned in 1911, and so they couldn't produce the negotiations and the discussions up to a treaty, what they call a treaty, which we call illegal. They're illegal under their own laws. They're illegal under our laws. Our laws has to have a council in order to determine any kind of transactions. Because of the destruction that was going through Haudenosaunee country years prior to that treaty-making time, we were still trying to rebuild other nations, so our leaders were out in Cayuga, Seneca, and helping rebuild after this scorched earth campaign of George Washington and Sullivan-Clinton campaign to destroy our villages.

**[23:07] Jake Edwards:** Our leaders were out there helping to rebuild. At the same time, New York State realized that and conducted some of these treaties without our leaders, and they appointed our young men as signers of a treaty. And it's sad to think back of what they used as coercion and bribery of money and fancy clothes and tools and different items that the young men wanted. And what they didn't realize was they were doing this all under the mindset of they got Negohadenios, rum, the mind changer.

**[23:54] Jake Edwards:** The mind changer was one of the highest purchased commodities from the state commissioners to deal with us. At any gathering, rum was always there. And so our young people, our young men that were sitting in these negotiations and discussions about land were under the influence of rum, and their minds were scattered. And they became, according to New York State, authorized to sign the documents. And so that's why the courts today won't let us use coercion, bribery, thievery, and false representation on signatures. All the main characteristics of what got them the jurisdiction over our land.

**[24:45] Betty Lyons Hill:** I wanted to ask too, because you were an integral part of putting in for our land rights action claim. You worked so hard and diligently on that. I myself have been working for the nation since 1997, and so I had the privilege and honor of watching you all work so hard on that and for it to not be accepted. Our land rights action that wasn't accepted by the Supreme Court. They wouldn't hear it. Wondering, do you think that we will ever realize a time that we will gain part of our lake back to help it heal?

**[25:20] Jake Edwards:** Well, yeah, I do. The lake and the shores of Onondaga Lake haven't gone anywheres. They've been abused as well as the original stewards of it, abused and sickened. And like I was talking about is what the rum did to our young men's minds was pretty much sickened their minds under the influence of rum. And so the transactions were fraudulent and coerced, not to count all the bribery that was in on it. And so when you look at the true history of how they obtained their jurisdiction over our lakes and then you get to the facts of the matters and how it came about, it should be very easy for them to help soothe the conscience of their minds and the future generations of their people coming that they'd done the right thing by turning over the land here at Onondaga Lake to the Onondagas, whether it's in whole or in joint usage.

**[26:16] Jake Edwards:** And looking at the history of how they used the land, they haven't respected Mother Earth the way it should be respected because Mother Earth still provides for us. So it should be in whole returned to the Onondaga Nation. My opinion of that and the records of how they obtained it coincide with each other with balance. So if we can fix the waters of Onondaga Lake is by doing the right thing, then let's look at the actual records of it that it belongs to Onondaga Nation. So when you say about returning it, it never left us. It's just somebody else's dirty jurisdiction is over it right now.

**[26:55] Betty Lyons Hill:** I think about also that humans are the only living being that contaminates their own environment and then thinks that they can go ahead and then drink the water. They contaminate the water that they think that they can drink. And getting the people in the surrounding area to understand how important it is to protect the lakes, and not just Onondaga Lake, which is our sacred lake, but also thinking about Lake Ontario that has Nine Mile Point sitting on it, that nuclear reactor that could potentially go awry. Our value system is very different. As Oren Lyons always says, like we need to-- they need a value system change for our mutual survival. How do you best see us doing that?

**[27:36] Jake Edwards:** Well, the same way what I just explained about the return of the jurisdiction around the lake and the waters is to follow the path of the destruction that you just mentioned about nuclear. It's not just a matter of a failed nuclear reactor. It's right now while they're mining the nuclear, and the waters in that area of where they're digging up the nukes right now. The spoils they leave behind, the contamination they cause throughout, it's not reported and it's not part of it. But so if you follow the facts of obtaining any kind of materials like that, whether it's fracked gas or oil, crude oil from Alberta or any of these commodities that they're benefiting largely on, price gouging at that, to the people. And so if you followed the facts on that, it would be easy to understand that to, in order to live in harmony and balance for future generations, you have to stop contaminating what you're walking on today.

**[28:37] Betty Lyons Hill:** I think about that balance a lot in our teachings and how im- there's such a grave imbalance that's happening now. What did you want listeners to remember from today? And what's the one thing that you would ask them to do?

**[28:52] Jake Edwards:** Well, there's a whole lot there, to get what you mentioned about cleaning the lake is for one thing is not just Onondaga Lake, but all the lake, all the waters. All the waters that you use, you must understand where they come from. Your drinking water, when you go to wash your car, any kind of waters that you use, where it comes from, what process does it go through to get to you, how you use it, and what you add to it before you put it to the drain, because downstream, some other being, whether it's a human being or an animal being or the trees or the insects, they're gonna be using that same water that you're already done using. So what you put in there is gonna affect the livelihood around us, and it'll throw off balance if there's something in that water not good for any of us.

**[29:50] Betty Lyons Hill:** And what would you ask them to do?

**[29:53] Jake Edwards:** I'd ask to be careful as to how you use any of the environment. You call it environment. We don't have a word for environment. But for all the beings, all our relatives, have respect for as you walk about here on Earth, on Mother Earth, and also acknowledge on a daily basis the gratitude that you should be sharing with our Mother, the Earth, so that she will provide for us.

**[30:21] Jake Edwards:** Because when you stop giving thanks, let's just say if you're getting a ride every day by the same guy, you're on your way to work, and you stop thanking him, he's gonna acknowledge that, or he's gonna notice that. This person's not even thankful anymore. There's, he's more demanding. Now he wants to go to the donut store first and not be a gratitude of it. So he's gonna stop giving you a ride. Well, it's the same concept in nature. When you stop giving thanks for the berries, they're gonna feel it, and they may not produce anymore. And our prophecies tell us that. They'll leave us when we stop giving thanks. So no matter how small the number of people there are giving thanks, I know somewhere there's at least one that's gonna give thanks for all of us. It's not just an individual thing. You're giving thanks for all of us, for all of us beings. It's to keep us happy and keep us content. That's our responsibility is sharing gratitude. That's how we were given that.

**[31:18] Betty Lyons Hill:** Thank you for coming in today. Thank you for answering these questions. I just wanted to say that you have made such a profound effect on my life. I've had the honor and privilege of being able to learn from you all of these years, so I want you to know how grateful I am for that because sometimes with the outside world and the false education we get, and the damage that the boarding schools have done, you didn't always get to learn what you should have maybe when you were younger. And so I just wanted to give you gratitude for imparting that on me as well. So thank you so much for your time today.
