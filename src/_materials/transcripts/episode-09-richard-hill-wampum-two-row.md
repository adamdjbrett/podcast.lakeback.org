We also cannot just sit in isolation in the woods or in our longhouse. We have to help our young people have the skills and the abilities to deal with racism, with colonization, with employment.

**[32:54] Richard W. Hill, Sr.:** So it's a new balancing act that I see the Two Row could provide. But again, everything's gotta be on deep reflection. You gotta think about this. You gotta take a look at what our ancestors did, and try to figure out where do we move forward from here. Our canoe's been severely damaged. The ship has grown to humongous size, and its wake often overwhelms us in our little canoe. Here's another thing they said, though. The Mohawk guy said, "It's true your people are gonna wanna get in our canoe, but I don't think they're gonna like it very much because it's too narrow and it goes down the river swiftly." It took me a long time to figure out what he's talking about. In fact, it wasn't until I capsized in the canoe and I realized that's what they're saying. In order to be in the canoe, you have to have absolute balance and harmony with everything in the canoe. You can't be dancing around and, you know, like on a ship, you can go on a cruise ship and you can do all kinds of crazy things. But in that canoe, that balance and harmony is necessary to keep the canoe upright between that sky world and that water world. It's almost as if life hangs in the balance, so you have to conduct yourself well. Does that make sense?

**[34:06] Adam DJ Brett:** That does. And it makes me think about what you said earlier about the right to catch a polluted fish doesn't do anyone any good. If you're in a canoe and it capsizes and the water's polluted and the fish are polluted, it doesn't do any good. So- how do we bring about then a healing between the Haudenosaunee indigenous peoples and the Europeans in their boats? Because it seems like treaty partners have perhaps stopped keeping their sides up. The chain has rusted. The waters are polluted. What does it look like to polish the covenant chain today, to heal the waters, and to move into the sort of people-to-people relationship that you have talked so much about throughout your life?

**[34:59] Richard W. Hill, Sr.:** Well, that's just it. I have no faith in the governments, the state, provincial, and federal government, changing course. They're just gonna keep plowing ahead. So our treaties with them, they've dropped the chain a long time ago. I don't even know if we still have a treaty, to tell you the truth. We believe so, but if your treaty partner doesn't believe it, where does it end up? But I think the real lesson here is that when you believed in the principles like the early colonists did, and they used the wampum and they spoke those words, that they were building a relationship.

**[35:34] Richard W. Hill, Sr.:** So the treaty's not a piece of parchment. It's not even a wampum belt. It is the nature of the relationship that you have to maintain and build upon those old concepts here of peace and goodwill and relationship to the Earth. So I believe all that we can do is to build those kind of partnerships, despite the government, despite industry, despite education. Like-minded people who are going to now try to follow the kind of instructions that we were given about treading lightly on the land and making sure there's something for the future generations and to develop that relationship.

**[36:13] Richard W. Hill, Sr.:** So I think that's the only way that we're going to make any headway, because if we try to get the United States to finally recognize the Canandaigua Treaty and live up to its articles, I don't know what that would look like, 'cause I... One of the primary elements of the Two Row and the Covenant Chain was- you have to have a mutual respect, and if you do, that would lead to developing trust. And with mutual respect and trust, then you can have ongoing friendship. I don't believe it's possible, now this is just me, I don't believe it's possible to have respect and trust with the state government or the federal government. So, I'm tired of batting my head against the wall hoping somehow they're gonna come to enlightenment. So instead, I've tried to find good-hearted people in which we develop these relationships, planting corn together, feasting together, getting our families together, and build what I believe was the real intention of treaty-making, was to extend the rafters of our longhouse to new family members.

**[37:16] Richard W. Hill, Sr.:** So that's, I think that's the way that I look at it. It's out of friendship that we can begin to address some of these matters. But can we have enough friends to stop the pollution of the waterways? I don't know. We're not gonna stop the people using cars, or all of that stuff that's causing the problem. But we can begin to, I believe, help our children and our grandchildren understand that they have a responsibility as a sacred being in the sacred world to act differently than maybe their grandparents did.

**[37:45] Adam DJ Brett:** As you're talking about these people-to-people relationships, having enough friends to make that impact and the difference, it reminded me about what you had said earlier about finding the wampums, helping the wampums come home. And so I was wondering, what has returning the wampum to the community taught you about returning land? And there's all of this talk about land back and indigenous lands in indigenous hands, but what I hear you saying is that there's something deeper, more fundamental than the notion of property ownership that has to be taken into account.

**[38:31] Richard W. Hill, Sr.:** Yeah, when you think about it, you know, the, the Mother Earth didn't run away. She's still always been there. She might be under a lot of concrete, but she's still there, and her power, her ability, and her function is still there. So I think that's about it, relating to that fact that if you wanna eat, guess where the food's gonna come from? An association with the Earth Mother and with the corn, beans, and squash, and the sun, the moon, the stars, the rain, all that stuff that we talk about in our Thanksgiving address. That's what allows you to continue to thrive here on, on the Earth. So we gotta get back to that. When I see what some of our people are doing to the land that we already have jurisdiction over, it's, it's, it's a crime in itself. So just getting land back's not enough. You have to change your thinking about what it is that matters to you the most because we, you know, we don't need another marijuana shop. We don't need another smoke shop. We don't need an AI terminal. We need to live as if the Earth matters because she does.

**[39:37] Richard W. Hill, Sr.:** So like land acknowledgment, it's turned into a big thing. We wanted it, and as soon as we get it, we don't want it, and now people are arguing over the function. But when you think about it, the Haudenosaunee have always been acknowledging the Mother Earth, always been acknowledging the plants that grow and the waters that flow, and the birds and the animal, all of that stuff. There's your land acknowledgment. But it was also not meant to be just a rote prayer or a rote speech. It's reminding you that you're doing these things, and when you do these things, this becomes evident. So I firmly believe that our future rests with how we relate to whatever land we have left and, and doing those things that our ancestors did. Now, at the same time, just like Onondaga Lake, we have to work cooperatively to recover it because it was one of the most toxic lakes in the United States, so that we can fulfill that function. That yes, the water is-- if, if it's true that water is life, then we have to call upon the power of the water to help restore its place in our lives. And, and I think once we do that, it just, to me it would seem like it changes so that you're not gonna be so wasteful.

**[40:50] Richard W. Hill, Sr.:** Now, I say this knowing, yeah, I live a modern life. I drive a car, you know, I have central heating and indoor plumbing. And I realize that how I live is part of the problem. So how do we mitigate the harm that we're doing? I remember I saw a movie one time about this woman recovering her roots to the people in Tibet, going back there, and she was amazed by watching the people on their pilgrimage, where they're crawling and they're going up to these shrines and making these prayers. But in her reflections on that, she says, "I hope when I get to be old and my grandchildren ask me, 'What have you done?' I can say that I did more than just pray." So it took me a while to understand what she was saying, but I get to appreciate that.

**[41:41] Richard W. Hill, Sr.:** Thanksgiving address is one thing, ceremonies are one thing, the Great Law is one thing, but it's how you conduct yourself in all of the days of the year, how you think about yourself, how you think about your relatives, how you think about the earth. That's what I'm talking about. Once you start doing that, you transform your thinking. That which you used to think was valuable kind of fades away, and then it takes the sweat equity of the planting, the harvesting, the making, the sharing, all of that kind of stuff so that we can live comfortably but also begin to minimize our carbon footprint on this land.

**[42:18] Adam DJ Brett:** One of the things I heard in what you were saying is that the move for land back is about a push for a restoration of a relationship, a reminder of a obligation and a responsibility towards balance and harmony. And that was going to require something of all of us, especially if we drive cars or have indoor plumbing, use all of the fancy technology. As I reflect on what you were saying, I began to think about how Onondaga Nation is asking for a piece of Onondaga Lake, particularly Maple Bay, to be returned so that they can begin to bring about a healing of the relationship. **[43:20] Richard W. Hill, Sr.:** Because we have this elemental philosophical and spiritual relationship to the thing we call our Mother, the Earth. It's been like parts of her have been imprisoned, taken away, off limits, and we just haven't been able to have the kind of daily relationship necessary to build a strong permanence. And the only way we can do that is by regaining jurisdiction over that piece of land. So I'm not talking about title and ownership because nobody can own the Earth, but I'm even cautious about stewardship or guardianship, but something like that because the Earth has been so abused, she has to go through this recovery. Because if you abuse yourself, you get addicted to bad things, you gotta remove all of those toxins from your system in order to be well. And I believe that's what the Earth is waiting for, indigenous people to reclaim that sacred relationship and help her renew her strength and her power to begin to make these kind of transformations that we're talking about that maybe go beyond human intellect. That there's something else that needs to take place.

**[44:26] Richard W. Hill, Sr.:** So I'm the kind of guy who said, I look at the record number of hurricanes, the forest fires, earthquakes. Where's that coming from? It's coming from the Earth. It's coming from all those elements that we give thanks for, but are maybe a little upset by our lack of commitment to follow through on our address of thanksgiving, so trying to get our attention. In our creation story, it talks about that. It says that they put the sun up in the, at noon the sun lingers to look down on the humans, see how they're doing. And they said if there's too much war going on, the sun lingers a little longer and turns up the thermostat to get your attention. So global climate change is because humans are killing too many humans around the world, and that the sun is trying to get our attention to say, "Get back to what really matters." Now, the scientists talk about us going past the tipping point where we can't change this environmental holocaust.

**[45:25] Richard W. Hill, Sr.:** But I have to believe that the power of the Earth and the sun and the moon and the stars and the thunder beings and all of that, which was created here on purpose, won't betray their purpose, but they need encouraging signs from humanity that we care enough to be more careful about how we live. And one of the problems with law and courts and land rights is that it's a very legalistic, depressing world. And I guess what I'm after is how do we get back to that historical joy that we had of being on that Onondaga Lake, harvesting the fish, just having community there. So that's the hope. That's what it can bring about. But it actually has to happen for everybody, not just us. You can't have a little environmental reservation, if you know what I mean, in a corner of the lake. It's the whole lake. It's everybody around the lake, but we gotta start somewhere. We gotta demonstrate the new possibility that gets people to think beyond New York State Fair and the big shopping mall, if you know what I mean.
