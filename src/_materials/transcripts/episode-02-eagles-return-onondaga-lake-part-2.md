## Conversation

**[01:17] Patricia Nolan Vogt:** My name is Patricia Nolan Vogt. I'm a descendant of the Onondaga Nation. I'm sitting here today with Tina Thomas and Sarah Shute.

**[01:26] Tina Thomas:** My name is Tina Thomas. I'm from the Onondaga Nation Eel Clan. I reside on the Onondaga Nation, and I'm partnering up with Sarah.

**[01:37] Sarah Shute:** Hi, my name is Sarah Shute. I'm from the Lafayette Tully area.

**[01:42] Patricia Nolan Vogt:** Sarah shared a little bit about some of the meaning the eagle has to Onondaga people. Can you share a little bit more, Tina? And only what you're comfortable in sharing.

**[01:54] Tina Thomas:** Like she said, it was in our great law of peace that the eagle sat at the top of the tree. This is part of the reason why our men wear the gustowa, the headpiece, and the feathers on top that identify who they are or where they're from are eagle feathers. So that's letting people know if they have them eagle feathers up there, then they are watching, they are protecting, they are trying to follow through with the natural instructions and our traditions and the way things were laid out for us. The eagle actually leaves a lot of gifts for us. They know when there is danger. They're the first ones to see it coming.

**[02:42] Tina Thomas:** So having them at the Skä•noñh center at the top of a tree is very significant because Sarah and I had to work, wake up activists for a couple of years that we, while we were there at the Skä•noñh center. And we had to play that, that part where we didn't want controversy, we didn't want all the arguing and fighting because the whole idea of the Great Law of Peace is talking and working these things out. So her and I had really tried without having to argue with anybody, just making reports, making phone calls, sending emails, and standing up to a lot of people to make sure that the eagles that sit there are protected.

**[03:30] Tina Thomas:** And I felt like it's, it was our turn to watch out for the, anything coming that was bad for the eagle. It was our turn to step up for them.

**[03:41] Patricia Nolan Vogt:** You both have spent years introducing visitors to the history at the Skä•noñh Center. What surprises them the most by the time they leave?

**[03:51] Tina Thomas:** I would say the connection that they have with all the beings around them, the natural world, and not knowing that they even had that connection. So when they leave, we would usually have a conversation at the counter, and the first thing they would go and grab is the Ganohé:nio, the book that has the Ganohé:nio, the words that they can say every day and wanting to build a relationship. I guess that would be the biggest thing that I noticed.

**[04:25] Sarah Shute:** Tina took the words out of my mouth. I feel exactly the same way. She's exactly right.

**[04:30] Patricia Nolan Vogt:** Yeah. You both have given us the importance of the eagle nest for being something that people can watch and care about. If Maple Bay were given back to the nation, what could grow there the same way?

**[04:44] Sarah Shute:** I think maybe looking at it from more of the human perspective and maybe a societal perspective and a precedent is this is an example of a place where ancestral lands were rightfully returned to the people who have been living there for time immemorial, for the people who honor the plants and the animals and the waters and who have a relationship with them. I certainly think much more than what I'm saying, but my feeling is this would set an example across Turtle Island of where this is not only possible, but where it's the right thing to do.

**[05:28] Tina Thomas:** I, my thoughts on giving land back that doesn't belong to somebody anyway in the first place is kinda on my ridiculous side, but when you say return to the Onondaga Nation, we actually never said we owned anything. It was a shared place. I believe our original words really say it all is it's a relationship for everyone to have with the lake. If you put it back in the care of the Onondaga Nation, we might be able to start thriving for a cleaner place, actually, for natural things to grow. We don't like to use the word steward too much, or I don't. I don't wanna steward anybody's land. I just want people to do the right thing and grow a relationship with the lands they're on. So I guess that would work for anybody, not just the Onondaga Nation. So if they don't want us to take care of it, at least take care of it themselves and build a relationship.

**[06:28] Patricia Nolan Vogt:** We shared about how driving past the Onondaga Lake how we felt. If you wanted to have someone understand more about it now, what would that be?

**[06:42] Tina Thomas:** Learn the history of your surroundings and not a made-up history, one that has been there for thousands of years. 'Cause no matter where you go in the countries, different countries, all countries there's Indigenous people. And for some reason, we all have a Kanahanya. We all have that relationship to place and land and an understanding relationship for one another. And I think people need to stop driving by and start stopping. Start cleaning up after yourselves. A lot of this is depressing when I see people put something on the ground as small as a quarter, but all you have to do is pick up where you stand, and that's a good start for any relationship.

**[07:29] Sarah Shute:** I think I would pick right up on what Tina said by saying slow down, which is a discussion that she and I had this morning on our drive out here, that Onondaga Lake Parkway in some times of the year has a speed limit of 55, I believe, and people go speeding down that parkway with a one-track mind needing to get to work as quickly as they can or needing to get to an appointment as quickly as they can. And especially in the winter, there are numerous bald eagles flying around. There's wildlife all over the parkway, and if we just slow down a little bit, you have more time to take all of that in. You realize that maybe what you have going on in that moment isn't necessarily the most important thing that's going on.

**[08:21] Sarah Shute:** And I would say to dig deeper beyond sort of the stories in your mind, right? When I talked in the beginning about, there's just, you're just told Onondaga Lake is the most polluted lake in the world, and you have this image of this grimy, smelly place. You almost envision like gree- green, weedy water. But that's not how Onondaga Lake looks. And so if you take the time to dig deeper beyond what you're told, you realize there's so much more going on. And so not only do you actually arrive at a really beautiful lake, Tina and I have seen some of the most beautiful sunsets that I've seen anywhere on the shores of Onondaga Lake.

**[09:09] Sarah Shute:** And when you dig deeper, this isn't just the most polluted lake in the world. This is the place where one of the most enduring examples of peace came together right on these very shores. And so that's what I hope that people do, that they slow down and then they look beyond what's on the surface and that they learn more about what really happened here and about the great importance of this area.

**[09:40] Patricia Nolan Vogt:** It's so important. For you both, what would you want listeners to remember or to do after hearing about the Eagles?

**[09:48] Tina Thomas:** I would go directly to the Ganohé:nio, the Thanksgiving Address. Go to the Skä•noñh Center. Read everything that's there. Listen and listen to the videos. Get an idea, find your connection. Learn how to connect and make a relationship with your surrounding beings.

**[10:07] Adam DJ Brett:** Those are the three things I think we should go out on. Patty, take us away.

**[10:10] Patricia Nolan Vogt:** You both have s- spoken a lot about the Ganahenia. Can you further elaborate on on that a little more?

**[10:20] Sarah Shute:** So at the center we refer to it as the Thanksgiving address, but there is also a translation or an understanding of the words that come before all else. And so this is a practice, this is a belief system, this is a way of life in which each day when you wake up, you can take the time to really recognize what's important and what it is that's sustaining our lives and human life and all beings on this planet, and express our gratitude for the waters, for the plants, for the animals, for the winds and the thunders and all the things that keep us alive, that keep us with, in relationship with one another.

**[11:15] Sarah Shute:** And when those relationships are damaged, we are damaged. And so as a personal practice, and this is what Tina and I were listening to and started to incorporate into our own lives when the eagles came back, I think it can change your mood, it can change your outlook from day to day and really in your own life. And so you can use that and think ahead seven generations. You can think ahead to the future, to your grandchildren, great-grandchildren and beyond, and think, "How can we leave this world better than we found it?" And it's through those practices.

**[11:59] Tina Thomas:** So my thoughts on the gunahanu is the words before all else. When you use this to build any relationship, we have to remember the first part that is mentioned is human beings. And I believe if you learn this Kanahán:niu, you share this Kanahán:niu, and you practice with a crowd, you practice with one other person, you practice with the beings around you, the trees, the grass, plants, the animals. This is a human way to get back to peace. It's a practice that has worked in the past, and it can be a practice that can work us back to peace in the future.

**[12:41] Patricia Nolan Vogt:** Which leads me into the final question. Speaking of those next seven generations, what can we do to educate our next seven generations on the importance of Mother Earth, the lake, the eagles, all of it?

**[13:00] Tina Thomas:** I'm gonna go right back to the teachings of the Ganeho'nyu. I do a cultural class, and it's usually once a week. So we downloaded a couple of things from the Play Store in Onondaga language, and it has the Ganeho'nyu right in there. So a lot of people that are assimilated are afraid to say our words out, out loud. I try to remind them, then do it when you're by yourself. Listen to it over and over. But if you have it on your phone, if you have it, if you just have the words and sharing the words and just help one another learn it, even if you're just listening to it, you still have that feeling of gratitude. And I think that's where everyone needs to start, is giving one another that acknowledgment of gratitude.

**[13:55] Sarah Shute:** I think for me, I do tend to be a person that spends a lot of time in my own thoughts, and I love being outside, but I can catch myself being outside and ruminating. And what I notice for myself and what I think could help others is the noticing, and like I said earlier, slowing down and noticing. So when I especially notice the things going on in the different seasons, that's something that helps me become more aware of what's happening with Mother Earth. So whether it's the first heavy rains and the thunders that come in the spring to wake the frogs and to wake the spring peepers, and the birds migrating and arriving in the spring and during May, and the insects and the grasshoppers and the crickets and the cicadas of the summer.

**[14:51] Sarah Shute:** When you slow down and you shut off the ruminating thoughts and you actually tune in to what's happening, you can feel yourself connecting more closely with those beings. And so what I have tried to start doing when I talk, whether it's kids or adults, and quite honestly, it's the adults that need a little more reminding than the kids, is to slow down, look, listen, and spend time with those beings, because if you really do listen, they will speak to you, and they will remind you that we are all connected. And so my biggest advice, which piggybacks right onto what Tina said, is to work on your relationship with the natural world.

**[15:41] Patricia Nolan Vogt:** I would like to say to both of you, not only for being here today and sharing your beau-beautiful words, but also for the love that you guys give, the wisdom that you guys share, education that you're willing to give out to our next generations is so important, and I appreciate both of you.

**[16:06] Sarah Shute:** It's really been a privilege, quite honestly, a life's privilege for what I experienced at the Skä•noñh Center, what I have learned there, how I have been able to share that wisdom with so many other people, for the friendships and the relationships that I have, and most especially for my dear friend Tina. I wouldn't wanna have my life without her.

**[16:31] Tina Thomas:** I would like to say Nya•weñha to both of you, and I'm going to cry now, so I'll leave it at that.
