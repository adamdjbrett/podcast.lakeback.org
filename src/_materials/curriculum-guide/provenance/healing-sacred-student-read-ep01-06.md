## Problems that run across the six episodes

1. **Ganohé:nio is spelled at least seven ways in the transcripts.** Episode 1 has "Kanahanyoh," "Kanahanu," "Kanahanyö" and "Kanahanio." Episode 2 has "Ganeho'nyu," "Kanahán:niu," "gunahanu," "Ganahenia" and "Kanahanya," alongside "Ganohé:nio." A student cannot tell these are one practice. Episode 1's Description uses "Ganohé:nio" and "the words that come before all else," but Part 1 never uses either.
2. **"Land rights action" and "land claim" are used for the same case, and the guide contradicts the speaker.** Episode 6 says "we tried to not say land claim," yet the guide's Episode 6 resource line describes "the 2005 federal land claim." The transcripts use "land rights action."
3. **No speaker labels.** Several questions and Descriptions attribute lines to a named speaker (Ep 1 Description, Ep 2 Q4 and Q5, Ep 3 Q5, Ep 6 Q4). I could not confirm those attributions from the transcripts.
4. **Several questions send the student outside the episode.** Ep 2 Q4, Ep 3 Q4, Ep 4 Q4 and Q5, and Ep 6 Q4 and Q5 each ask for a listed page, booklet or guide resource before the question can be answered.
5. **Three Descriptions say the episode "leaves a class asking" a question** (Ep 4, Ep 5, Ep 6). No transcript says this. It reads as if the speakers said it.

## Episode 1: The Eagles Return to Onondaga Lake, Part 1

### Before listening

- Words I did not understand: Skä•noñh; Haudenosaunee; Great Law of Peace; Thanksgiving Address; Ganohé:nio; bioaccumulation; Eel Clan and Snipe Clan (I know "clan" is a family group, not what it means here); Onondaga Nation (I could not tell whether this is a place or a people); Superfund (in the resource list).
- Expected topic: two women who worked at a cultural center on Onondaga Lake talk about the lake's pollution and about eagles nesting there.

### After listening

- Matches: the Syracuse west-side childhood and "Stay out of the water"; the snapping-turtle toenail study; the 2020 mornings with the Address; the 2022 first recorded nest; people trying to climb the tree; "resilience."
- Not in the transcript:
  - "Skä•noñh is an Onondaga greeting meaning 'Peace and Wellness'." The phrase "Peace and Wellness" does not appear.
  - "Ganohé:nio, 'the words that come before all else'." Part 1 never uses Ganohé:nio. It says "Kanahanyoh" and "Kanahanio," and the phrase "words that come before all else" is in Part 2, not Part 1.
  - The Description gives specific claims to Shute (toxins building up in the fish, "resilience") and to Thomas, but the transcript has no speaker labels.
- Left out, and needed: the eel. Thomas says "an eel is the first animal to leave the water," and Question 4 depends on that idea. The photographers' role is also missing, though Question 5 depends on it. The Description does not mention the "Salt City" or the 1929 replanting, both of which Thomas raises.

### Discussion questions

**Q1.** How did Tina Thomas's and Sarah Shute's understanding of Onondaga Lake change as they learned more about its cultural and environmental significance?
Both moved from distance to attention. Shute says "I wasn't taught much about the lake except for I remember it smelling," and only later "became much more aware of the mercury contamination" through her consulting work. Thomas says "it wasn't until my 40s that I found out that this area is the home of democracy," which she came to value more than the lake's Salt City name. Shute says the lake was "one of the most beautiful places I had ever been," which reversed what she had expected.
Ratings: answerable yes; clear yes; leading no; think.

**Q2.** What does the return of bald eagles to Onondaga Lake mean to Thomas and Shute, and what concerns remain about the lake's environmental health?
The birds mean resilience: one speaker says "the takeaway was resilience," because the eagles nest in a busy, high-traffic place and still succeed. The concerns are contamination that stays in the lake: "there still are toxic sediments," and the fish the birds eat. Thomas's test for the lake's health is the eel, and "if you don't see a eel, then you know it's still not clean." The answer is fully in the transcript.
Ratings: answerable yes; clear yes; leading no; think.

**Q3.** How does the Skä•noñh Center use the history of Onondaga Lake and the Great Law of Peace to teach visitors about cooperation, peace, and environmental responsibility?
The tour begins outside, at the lake, because Tina's idea was "to say, 'Look at this lake, because we're about to tell you a part of the history that happened here.'" It ends at the Great Law exhibit, where people "resolve conflict" through "the power of the good mind." Cooperation and peace are explicit. Environmental responsibility is not taught as a lesson in the transcript; the closest line, "We definitely focused on how terrible everything was," describes the tour, not what visitors should do. The third part is partly answerable at most.
Ratings: answerable partly; clear yes; leading yes (it names all three lessons it expects); think.

**Q4.** Thomas reads the lake's health through the eel, whose absence tells her the water is "still not clean." Shute read it through lab tests on snapping-turtle toenails. Compare these two ways of knowing whether a lake is healthy. What can each notice that the other might miss?
Thomas's eel is a living signal: "an eel is the first animal to leave the water, toxic waters, poison waters." Shute's samples were tissue from snapping turtles, "the contamination in the snapping turtles" being "significantly higher than turtles from other surrounding wetlands." The transcript gives both methods but never compares them, so the part about what each might miss is the student's own inference. The question is partly answerable for that reason.
Ratings: answerable partly; clear yes; leading no; think.

**Q5.** The eagles drew photographers whose behavior at first endangered the nest and later helped protect it. Using this case, explain how public attention can both harm and help a recovering place. Who should decide how close the public may come, and on what grounds?
The harm is in the transcript: "There were people going under the tree," "people driving up right to the trunk of the tree and trying to get photographs," and "We've even seen people try to climb the tree." The help came when photographers "became very protective of the eagles" and kept others back. Shute's summary, "I think people are mostly well-meaning but sometimes when you're well-meaning, you can still cause harm," covers the "how." The transcript does not say who should decide, so that half is the student's opinion.
Ratings: answerable partly; clear yes; leading no; think.

### Suggestion

Add the eel to the Description: "Thomas reads the lake's health through the eel, which she calls 'the first animal to leave the water.'" Question 4 depends on it, and right now a student meets "the eel" for the first time in the question.

## Episode 2: The Eagles Return to Onondaga Lake, Part 2

### Before listening

- Words I did not understand: Maple Bay (I could not tell what it is or where); Turtle Island; "steward"; seven generations; Ganohé:nio (again); headpiece; parcel; precedent; LakeBack (a campaign name, I assume); "ancestral lands" and "rightfully returned"; Kayanerenkó:wa (resource list).
- Expected topic: the eagle's meaning in the Great Law, a proposal to return land at Maple Bay, and closing advice on gratitude.

### After listening

- Matches: the Great Law's "talking and working these things out"; Shute's "Turtle Island" precedent; Thomas's dislike of "steward"; the weekly class and the Onondaga-language apps; the Parkway slow-down advice.
- Not in the transcript:
  - "a parcel on the northwest shore of Onondaga Lake." The transcript says only "Maple Bay." The location is not given.
  - "the eagle sits atop the Tree of Peace." The phrase "Tree of Peace" does not appear in Part 2 (it is in Part 1). The transcript says "the eagle sat at the top of the tree."
- Left out, and needed: what Maple Bay is. The transcript never says. The Description asks "what would returning Maple Bay change?" without giving the student anything to answer it. Also left out: "learn the history of your surroundings," which Question 3 is built on, and "pick up where you stand."

### Discussion questions

**Q1.** What does the eagle represent in the Great Law of Peace, and how does its role as a guardian connect to Thomas and Shute's efforts to protect the eagles at the Skä•noñh Center?
The eagle "sat at the top of the tree" in the Great Law, and it sees danger first: "They know when there is danger. They're the first ones to see it coming." Eagle feathers on the men's headpieces mean "they are watching, they are protecting." The speakers took on the same job at the Center: "it was our turn to step up for them." The connection the question asks for is in the transcript.
Ratings: answerable yes; clear yes; leading no; think.

**Q2.** How do Thomas and Shute describe the Thanksgiving Address as a way of building relationships with the natural world? In what ways might people bring these teachings into daily life?
The Address is a daily practice: "each day when you wake up, you can take the time to really recognize what's important," and "express our gratitude for the waters, for the plants, for the animals." One speaker's first step is to "Learn how to connect and make a relationship with your surrounding beings." Shute says to slow down along the Parkway, and Thomas adds "slow down, look, listen, and spend time with those beings." The speakers also say to "think ahead seven generations."
Ratings: answerable yes; clear yes; leading no; think.

**Q3.** Why do Thomas and Shute stress learning the Indigenous history of the places where we live? How might a relationship with the land change the way people treat it?
The reason given is that Indigenous people are everywhere: "all countries there's Indigenous people." The history is to be learned as it is: "Learn the history of your surroundings and not a made-up history, one that has been there for thousands of years." On treatment, Thomas says "I just want people to do the right thing and grow a relationship with the lands they're on," and the speakers say the first step is to "pick up where you stand." The speakers do claim that the relationship changes treatment, and the transcript supports that claim.
Ratings: answerable yes; clear yes; leading no; think.

**Q4.** Asked about Maple Bay, Shute speaks of a precedent for "ancestral lands" being "rightfully returned," while Thomas says the Onondaga "never said we owned anything" and resists the word "steward." Explain the difference between these two answers. How does each fit with the case for Maple Bay made in the AILA and LakeBack materials?
Taking the question's attributions as given, the first answer frames Maple Bay as land returned to people who "honor the plants and the animals," and the second frames it as "a shared place": "we actually never said we owned anything. It was a shared place." Thomas adds "I don't wanna steward anybody's land." The first half is answerable. The second half asks about "the case for Maple Bay made in the AILA and LakeBack materials," which the episode does not describe. A student who does not open those pages cannot finish the question.
Ratings: answerable partly; clear no (Maple Bay is never explained, and "the case" points to materials the student has not seen); leading no; look up.

**Q5.** Thomas says she and Shute tried to protect the eagles "without having to argue with anybody," in keeping with the Great Law. Explain how that commitment shaped what they did. What are the strengths and limits of this approach in a dispute over a shared place?
The commitment shaped their work: "her and I had really tried without having to argue with anybody just making reports, making phone calls, sending emails." The strength is the Great Law's method, "the whole idea of the Great Law of Peace is talking and working these things out." The limits are not discussed; the transcript describes no argument they lost and no moment where the method failed. A "dispute over a shared place" is also not described, since Maple Bay is raised as a question rather than as a dispute the speakers joined.
Ratings: answerable partly; clear yes; leading no; think.

### Suggestion

Add one sentence on what Maple Bay is, using the guide's own Maple Bay resource description ("a parcel on Onondaga Lake that LakeBack wants returned"). Question 4 and the Description's closing question both depend on it, and the transcript never says what it is. Also drop "northwest shore," which the transcript does not support.

## Episode 3: Jake Edwards on Onondaga Lake and the Ganohé:nio

### Before listening

- Words I did not understand: Ganohé:nio; Superfund (the Description glosses it); Crucible Steel; Doctrine of Discovery; "quasi-treaties"; "land rights action"; seventh generation; Edward Everett (a name); Seventh Generation Fund and Indigenous Values Initiative (board roles); Solvay Process; Honeywell; unceded; Turtle Island (resource list).
- Expected topic: a man recalls the lake's pollution from his childhood, then talks about land taken by New York State and the Doctrine of Discovery.

### After listening

- Matches: the late-1960s memory and "yellow-orangish-green sludge"; Crucible Steel; the whitefish "you could walk across their backs"; "I am the environment"; "there's no room for words of contamination"; the Edward Everett report; "it never left us."
- Not in the transcript:
  - "(a federally listed hazardous-waste cleanup site)." This gloss is the guide's own. The transcript says only "it's still a Superfund site."
  - "New York State 'quasi-treaties' signed by young men plied with rum." Close, but the transcript's "young men" line is paraphrased. The Description reads more smoothly than the speaker does.
- Left out, and needed for Question 4: the 1911 Albany fire that, the speaker says, destroyed the state's records; the "treaty-making time of 1788"; and the land rights action's rejection by the Supreme Court. Also left out: the fish kill, the "$500 million" remediation, and the 1922 ban on ceremony at the lake.

### Discussion questions

**Q1.** How did Jake Edwards's childhood experiences and his uncle's teachings shape his understanding of Onondaga Lake and the effects of contamination?
Edwards's childhood gave him sights: "that pipe that went across the highway," and "a whole bunch of fish floating belly up." His uncle gave him the explanation: "He used to work at Crucible Steel, and he said he knows what was being put in" the water. The uncle's stories of whitefish "so abundant in fish that you could walk across their backs" gave Edwards the lake's earlier condition. The uncle's line "I am the environment" is what Edwards carries into his view of contamination.
Ratings: answerable yes; clear yes; leading no; think.

**Q2.** How does the Great Law of Peace ask people to weigh the consequences of their decisions for future generations and the natural world?
The law asks that "the decisions that we make are to no way negatively affect seven generations coming." It also sets up ways to settle disagreement: "there is mechanisms put in place to deal with those different feelings and different ideas." The transcript adds, "We acknowledge all the humans and all the beings." The answer is in the transcript.
Ratings: answerable yes; clear yes; leading no; think.

**Q3.** Why does Edwards connect the return of Onondaga Lake to the Onondaga Nation with environmental healing? How might recognizing Indigenous relationships with the land change approaches to environmental protection?
Edwards says the lake should be left to heal: "Take out what you put in there and let it be, let it recover." He says "the water has its own rights," and that tourism on the lake keeps it from healing: "Don't crowd it out with what caused it in the first place." The first half is in the transcript. The second half asks how recognizing these relationships would change protection, which the episode does not argue. The student has to extend what Edwards says.
Ratings: answerable partly; clear yes; leading no; think.

**Q4.** Explain how Edwards links the way New York State acquired Onondaga land in the late 1700s to who controls Onondaga Lake today. Compare his oral account with the Onondaga Nation's own statements on its Land Rights pages or in its complaint. What does each kind of record contribute?
Edwards links the land takings to the "treaty-making time of 1788," when young men signed "quasi-treaties" under the influence of rum, and to the state's loss of records: "The records of those talks and policies were destroyed in the fire of, 1911 in Albany." His conclusion is "it never left us." The transcript does not say "late 1700s," but 1788 falls in that period. The second half, comparing his account with the Nation's Land Rights pages and complaint, cannot be done from the episode.
Ratings: answerable partly; clear no ("oral account," "Land Rights pages," and "complaint" are not explained, and "the Nation's own statements" is unclear); leading no; look up.

**Q5.** Edwards's uncle refused the label "environmentalist" because "I am the environment," and Edwards adds, "We don't have a word for environment." Explain how this view differs from treating the lake as a place for recreation, as Betty Hill (Lyons) describes the bike paths and boating. Apply Edwards's closing advice to one water source you use: where does it come from, and who uses it after you?
The contrast is in the transcript: the lake is a relative, but the paths and boating treat it as a place to use. The speaker of the bike-path lines is not labeled, so the attribution to Hill cannot be confirmed. The closing advice is in the transcript: "All the waters that you use, you must understand where they come from." The application to a water source is the student's own work. The question asks three things at once.
Ratings: answerable partly; clear yes (the words are clear, but the question packs three tasks into one); leading no; think.

### Suggestion

Split Question 4. Its first half, how the 1788 treaty-making links the state's land takings to the lake today, can be answered from the episode. Its second half, comparison with the Nation's pages and complaint, needs those pages named in the question.

## Episode 4: Rex Lyons on Lacrosse and the Haudenosaunee Nationals, Part 1

### Before listening

- Words I did not understand: "Gaeñ hia uh" (the host's name); "De Ho Wih Da Dis" (the guest's name); medicine game; secular game; Creator's game; cradle stick; Medtronic (a company); exhibition game; full member; sovereignty; Haudenosaunee Nationals; Iroquois Nationals; Alfie Jacques Ambassador Award; Roy Simmons Jr.; Louis Jock and Rick Hill and Wes Patterson (names).
- Expected topic: a man on lacrosse as a tradition, then on the founding of the team.

### After listening

- Matches: the cradle stick photo; the stickmakers; "a living, breathing thing at one time"; "There's no refs, but there are rules"; "I end up talking about the environment"; the Iroquois Nationals' founding; "an exhibition game."
- Not in the transcript: "The episode leaves a class asking: what changes when a game is also a relationship with the natural world?" No speaker asks a class anything. This is the guide's own question.
- Left out, and needed: the medicine game's details (burned tobacco, "your intention is everything") are not in the Description, which names the medicine game without explaining it. Also left out: the river-and-current lesson, the schooling critique (which Key Topics names but the Description barely covers), and the "Dream Team."

### Discussion questions

**Q1.** How does Rex Lyons describe the cultural and spiritual significance of lacrosse in Haudenosaunee communities, and how does that understanding differ from treating lacrosse only as a competitive sport?
Lyons says "I understand the difference between the secular game and the medicine game." The medicine game is ceremony: the players "burned tobacco and they've started the ceremony," and then "play as hard as you can, but fair." What separates the two, for him, is intention: "your intention is everything." The competitive game is the secular one, and the difference is in purpose, not in the rules.
Ratings: answerable yes; clear yes; leading no; think.

**Q2.** The episode stresses relationships with the natural world. How does this perspective challenge dominant Western approaches to education, technology and environmental responsibility?
Lyons criticizes schooling: children are taught to "Stay between the lines," to become "a good little worker bee," and he says "you're doing your students a huge disservice by not educating them to their relationship and dependence upon the natural world." On technology he worries that AI could "go rogue." On the environment he names "that disconnect of understanding the balance between the human beings and the natural world." The word "Western" is the question's, not his.
Ratings: answerable yes; clear yes; leading yes (the question assumes the perspective challenges Western approaches); think.

**Q3.** What is the significance of the Haudenosaunee Nationals for self-determination, sovereignty and cultural identity? How does the team's founding show the relationship between sport and cultural preservation?
Lyons says an international team would "implement our self-determination and our sovereignty." Joining as a "fully sovereignly recognized nation" was the result. On identity he says "lacrosse is much deeper to us" and "It's in our DNA." The founding shows the link between sport and culture in his account, though the question's phrase "show the relationship" assumes the answer.
Ratings: answerable yes; clear yes; leading yes; think.

**Q4.** Lyons recalls early talk of an "exhibition game" for the Haudenosaunee rather than full membership in the international federation. What did the team's founders gain, and what did they take on, by insisting on full member status? Use his account of the early years and the Haudenosaunee Nationals site.
Lyons describes the exhibition game as something "on some kind of a lesser plane," and full membership as "being an equal." What they gained was that recognition. What they took on was years of losing: "we lost a lot of games, but we were never defeated," and "all those hours, of all of the money, he had to raise." The question's second half needs "the Haudenosaunee Nationals site," which the question does not name and the episode does not describe.
Ratings: answerable partly; clear no ("international federation" and "the Haudenosaunee Nationals site" are not explained); leading no; think.

**Q5.** Lyons says many non-Native players feel the game's origins but "don't have the instruction that goes with it." Using the episode and the Alfie Jacques Ambassador Award and Roy Simmons Jr. pages, explain what he thinks instruction adds to the game. Then apply his distinction to another practice that has passed from one culture into wide use.
From the episode: instruction gives "the significance of the spiritual origins and the medicine game," and players who understand "your intention is everything." The Alfie Jacques and Roy Simmons pages are required by the question, and the episode does not describe them. The "distinction" the question asks the student to apply is not stated clearly; I could not tell whether it means the feeling and the instruction, or the sport and the ceremony.
Ratings: answerable partly; clear no; leading no; look up.

### Suggestion

Gloss the two games in the Description, in one sentence drawn from the episode: the medicine game is the ceremonial version, played after burned tobacco, where "your intention is everything"; the secular game is the ordinary sport. Questions 1 and 5 depend on the distinction, and the Description names it without explaining it.

## Episode 5: Rex Lyons on Lacrosse and the Haudenosaunee Nationals, Part 2

### Before listening

- Words I did not understand: clan mother; longhouse; Perth (a place; I assume the World Games); Haudenosaunee passports; Tree of Peace; "Olympic inclusion"; birthplace of lacrosse; governance; stewardship; "reciprocity"; environmental justice; sovereignty; "international bodies"; Organization of American States and petition (resource list).
- Expected topic: what representing the team asks of a person, the flag and anthem, and the Olympic campaign.

### After listening

- Matches: the clan mother's lesson; the flag and anthem in Perth; the IOC meeting in Switzerland; the Tree of Peace line "represents universal laws that govern all life."
- Not in the transcript, or not as described: "The conversation ends with Onondaga Lake as the birthplace of lacrosse and the Tree of Peace." The lake and the Tree of Peace come about two-thirds of the way in. The conversation ends with the garden, "we move at the speed of pain," and thanks.
- Left out, and needed: Captain Pratt's "Kill the Indian, save the man," and "generational trauma" (which the Description omits, though Question 4 leans on the "genocidal practices" line); the clan mothers' power to remove leaders (the closest the transcript comes to "real democracy"); Michael Kennedy's Alfie Jacques Award; and "dollars and cents over common sense," the speaker's critique of the cleanup.

### Discussion questions

**Q1.** How does Rex Lyons describe the responsibilities of a member of the Haudenosaunee Nationals? How does this challenge conventional Western understandings of athletic achievement and individual success?
The responsibility begins with a clan mother's warning: "It's not yours to just do with as you please." He adds that "They wanna play just like you," so the players are watched. Representing the nation means "carrying the nationhood on my shoulders," and his earlier, individual view of the game was "very one-dimensional." The contrast with individual achievement comes from the speaker. The "Western" label is the question's.
Ratings: answerable yes; clear yes; leading yes ("How does this challenge" assumes a challenge); think.

**Q2.** What connections does the conversation draw between Haudenosaunee governance, cultural identity and environmental stewardship? How might these inform contemporary approaches to leadership and sustainability?
The governance link is direct: the clan mothers, "choosing our leaders." The identity link runs through the Tree of Peace, "universal laws that govern all life." On the environment he says the laws must be respected: "you have to know those laws, respect those laws, and work within those laws." The word "stewardship" is not in the episode, and the contemporary application is the student's extension.
Ratings: answerable partly; clear yes; leading no; think.

**Q3.** Why is the campaign to protect and reclaim Onondaga Lake significant to Haudenosaunee communities? How does it show the relationship between environmental justice, cultural preservation and Indigenous sovereignty?
Lyons calls the lake "sacred to us" and talks about the campaign's youth focus. His environmental point is that the cleanup has been poor: "I didn't care that my generations after me are gonna have to clean my mess up... that's criminal." The question names "environmental justice" and "Indigenous sovereignty," which the transcript does not use, so the three-way relationship is the student's to build.
Ratings: answerable partly; clear no ("environmental justice" is never explained); leading yes ("How does it show the relationship" assumes the link); think.

**Q4.** Lyons describes the Olympic campaign as "trying to participate in something that's not ours, but they're taking something that is ours." Explain the tension he names. What would reciprocity, the word he reaches for, look like in this case, and what might stand in its way?
The tension is in the quote: "trying to participate in something that's not ours, but they're taking something that is ours." The word he uses is "reciprocate," not "reciprocity": "what is being done to res- reciprocate that?" He wants "everybody should win, not just one side or the other." What stands in the way, in his account, is "these genocidal practices," and he points to Captain Pratt's "Kill the Indian, save the man."
Ratings: answerable yes; clear no (the question says he uses "reciprocity," but the transcript has "reciprocate"); leading no; think.

**Q5.** Lyons says that before Perth, "We don't have flags. We don't have anthems," and that the team "had to do all this stuff on the fly." Compare these new symbols with the older sources of instruction he describes, such as the clan mother and the Tree of Peace. What does his account suggest about how, and how far, a nation can take on the forms that international bodies require?
The new symbols were built to meet a requirement: "If we wanna participate in this thing, this is what you need." The older sources, the clan mother and the Tree of Peace, are about responsibility and universal law. So his account suggests a nation can take on the required forms while its older instruction continues. He does not say how far that can go, so the "how far" half is not answered in the transcript.
Ratings: answerable partly; clear yes; leading no; think.

### Suggestion

Change "reciprocity" in Question 4 to "reciprocate," the word Lyons actually uses. The question says he "reaches for" a word the transcript does not contain, which sends a student looking for it.

## Episode 6: Tadodaho Sidney Hill on the Peacemaker and Onondaga Lake

### Before listening

- Words I did not understand: Tadodaho (the title); Haudenosaunee Confederacy; Peacemaker; Gayaneshkaho; the five nation names (Mohawks, Oneidas, Onondagas, Cayugas, Senecas); land rights action; Doctrine of Discovery; Declaration; sovereign; Grand Council and "motion to dismiss" (resource list); "Neighbor to Neighbor, Nation to Nation" (a booklet title); environmental injustice (Question 2).
