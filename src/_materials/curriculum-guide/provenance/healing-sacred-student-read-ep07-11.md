**Q1.** Hill says the Great Law is "a kind of a misnomer" and calls it a way of thinking and a mirror. How does he describe the Great Law as more than a written law, and what roles do relationships, personal reflection and collective responsibility play in the peace he describes?

*Answer.* Hill says "even the law is a kind of a misnomer. It's really a way of thinking." *Ratings.* Answerable: yes. Clear: no ("misnomer" is quoted and not explained). Leading: no. Think.

**Q2.** Hill calls the wampum strings "our history book" and "our Constitution," and he tells listeners to read treaty minutes rather than only the signed parchment. How does he distinguish the knowledge carried in wampum and oral tradition from the knowledge in written colonial records? What role does wampum play in diplomacy and treaty-making in his account?

*Answer.* Hill says "there's our history book, there's our Constitution, there's our way of life. It's all kind of laid out in these wampum strings." On written records, he says the meaning is "not necessarily in the parchment that was signed at the end," and he gives the translation problem as one reason. On diplomacy he says "The wampum was the diplomatic protocol, and everybody understood it, that when you speak, you have to hold it." The contrast with written records is only partly drawn, and the line "just because it's written doesn't mean it's true" is the host's memory of Hill's earlier talk, not something said in this episode.

*Ratings.* Answerable: partly. Clear: no ("treaty minutes" and "oral tradition" are not explained). Leading: no. Think.

**Q3.** How does Hill interpret the Two Row's image of the canoe and the ship traveling together? How does the fuller narrative he tells, including what happens to people who move between vessels or stand with a foot in each, change the way the metaphor can be read?

*Answer.* Hill reads the Two Row as a rule about law rather than race: "you abide by the rules, the law of the vessel that you're in." He reframes the common view that "some people look at the Two Row as a racial barrier." The fuller narrative adds that someone with "one foot in a canoe and one foot in a ship" would fall, since "they're gonna push apart, you're gonna fall into deep water." Hill's own use is "strengthen the canoeness in your life," meaning staying rooted in one's own law when going into the other.

*Ratings.* Answerable: yes. Clear: yes. Leading: no. Think.

**Q4.** Hill argues that a treaty is "not a piece of parchment" and "not even a wampum belt" but a relationship that needs respect, trust and friendship. How does this differ from conventional Western legal or governmental approaches to treaties, and what does Hill propose when a treaty partner no longer keeps up the relationship?

*Answer.* Hill says "the treaty's not a piece of parchment. It's not even a wampum belt," and that "It is the nature of the relationship" that has to be kept. The episode does not describe Western legal approaches to treaties, so the comparison is only partly answerable; the closest it comes is that the state and federal governments "look at everything as a monetary value." When a partner stops keeping up, Hill says "I have no faith in the governments ... changing course," so he looks for "good-hearted people in which we develop these relationships." His remedy is practical, "planting corn together, feasting together," not a legal process.

*Ratings.* Answerable: partly. Clear: yes. Leading: no. Think.

**Q5.** Hill recounts a judge in the Cayuga land rights action saying that pristine land has no value, and he says that "just getting land back's not enough." Using his contrast between the Earth as sacred and land as economic value, explain what he thinks returning land, such as Maple Bay on Onondaga Lake, would restore that returning property would not.

*Answer.* Hill quotes the judge: "Pristine land has no value," adding that value appears only "when you put in a road, put in a fence." He sets the contrast directly: "Our philosophy of the sacredness of life versus the continual industrialization and commercialization of the Earth." He then says "So just getting land back's not enough," because the return means "regaining jurisdiction over that piece of, land," and "nobody can own the Earth," so a property transfer would not restore the relationship. The last clause of the question, "would restore that returning property would not," is garbled, so I answered the question as I read it: what does returning land restore that a transfer of property would not?

*Ratings.* Answerable: yes (once the garbled clause is read as intended). Clear: no (the last clause cannot be parsed). Leading: no. Think.

**Suggestion, Episode 9.** Fix Question 5. Its last clause ("would restore that returning property would not") cannot be parsed, and a single sentence such as "What does returning land restore that a transfer of property would not?" would let the question stand.

**Q1.** How did Haudenosaunee governance and social organization shape Gage's understanding of women's rights and equality, as Eaton describes it? Which features of Haudenosaunee society challenged the assumptions of the settler society Gage lived in?

*Answer.* Eaton says Gage found in the Haudenosaunee "the perfect example of this egalitarian society that she was looking for." The features that challenged settler assumptions include that "Women owned their own property" and that "Women had their own place and their own knowledge that was respected." The settler view, Eaton says, was that "Because in the white community, women were to have babies," and "That was their only purpose." After childbearing age, settler women were "writing them off," which is the direct contrast with Haudenosaunee respect for older women.

*Ratings.* Answerable: yes. Clear: yes. Leading: no. Think.

**Q2.** What is the difference between matriarchy and matrilineal descent as Eaton explains them? How do the two ideas help us understand the roles of women in Haudenosaunee society?

*Answer.* Eaton defines matrilineal descent narrowly: "women keep the clan so that as a child is born, that child belongs to that clan." Matriarchy, by contrast, is "a much wider system," pictured as a circle with the protectors outside and "children, the elderly, the sick, the disabled, and nature" at the center. She says that "some societies have matrilineal, lines without having a matriarchy. Some have both." Together the terms show that women held clan membership, and that the circle puts nurturing life at the center, which is how she explains women's roles.

*Ratings.* Answerable: yes. Clear: yes. Leading: no. Think.

**Q3.** Why does Eaton stress oral history and the recovery of overlooked stories? How might recognizing Haudenosaunee women's influence change the way the history of the women's rights movement is told?

*Answer.* Eaton says the historical community once ruled oral history out: "Well, if we can't find it written down, then it can't be true." She explains that if "you can find multiple people who are telling the same story in different areas... we cross-reference them, they're proving themselves." She argues that recognizing Haudenosaunee influence means "we can correlate those stories to evidence," and that historians are now "valuing those oral histories much higher than we used to." How the movement's history would be retold is only partly answered, because she describes a change in method rather than a new account of the movement.

*Ratings.* Answerable: yes. Clear: yes. Leading: no. Think.

**Q4.** Eaton presents the argument, developed by Sally Roesch Wagner, that Haudenosaunee women influenced early suffragists such as Lucretia Mott and Gage. What kinds of evidence does the episode point to for this argument (for example, Gage's published book, Elizabeth Cady Stanton's writing, accounts of Mott's visits, and oral histories)? What can each kind of evidence show, and what would it leave open? Use the listed resources to test your answer.

*Answer.* The episode points to Gage's book, which Eaton says she researched "for 30 years." It points to Stanton's writing, since "Elizabeth Cady Stanton wrote about this because she loved it so much" and wrote about it "like five or six different times." It points to oral histories, which Eaton says can be cross-referenced. Its one example of Mott's visits is an assertion without a source: "she regularly went in to these festival events." Each kind of source shows what its writer saw and admired, but the episode does not say what each would leave open or how to test it, and Eaton herself says the idea "hasn't even been heard." I did not use the listed resources, because the assignment limits me to the transcript.

*Ratings.* Answerable: partly. Clear: no (the question mixes a list of evidence with an instruction to use resources, and "accounts of Mott's visits" refers to nothing the episode cites). Leading: yes (mild): the parenthetical hands over the list of evidence types. Think.

**Q5.** Using Eaton's account of coverture, compare what a settler wife and a Haudenosaunee wife could do when a marriage ended, in terms of property, home and children. Why might that comparison have mattered so much to Stanton and Gage, who wanted to change the law and not only win the vote?

*Answer.* Under coverture, Eaton says that if a woman "wanted to take her children with her, she could not. They belonged to her husband," and "She had no claim over the house that was theirs." In the Haudenosaunee case, a wife "could take his stuff and put it outside, then they're divorced," and "she keeps the children, she keeps the house." Property, home and children all differ between the two. Eaton says Stanton "wrote about it like five or six different times," and that the women decided that "if they could get the right to vote, then they could get in and actually change the legal system, and that was the goal for Matilda."

*Ratings.* Answerable: yes. Clear: yes. Leading: no. Think.

**Suggestion, Episode 10.** Rewrite Question 4 so it does not list example evidence and asks the student to name the evidence the episode gives. The list includes "accounts of Mott's visits," which the episode does not cite as a source.

**Discussion questions.**

**Q1.** How did Gage's relationship with the Haudenosaunee shape her views on women's rights and on Haudenosaunee sovereignty?

*Answer.* Gage was "considering" for the Council of Mothers, and Eaton says that "was able to participate at the highest levels of government, where she was a non-entity in her own country." Her view of sovereignty is in her defense of the treaties: "No, they are a sovereign nation. They, we have treaties with this nation." Eaton adds that the treaties were also made with women: "The great mothers were part of those treaties." The word sovereignty is not defined in this section, although the transcript explains the claim through the treaties.

*Ratings.* Answerable: yes. Clear: no ("sovereignty" is undefined). Leading: no. Think.

**Q2.** What differences does the episode describe between Haudenosaunee views of women and the views of the dominant Christian settler society in Gage's lifetime?

*Answer.* The central contrast in the episode is that "women were very much seen as something to fight against, whereas in the Haudenosaunee stories, women were something to uphold." Eaton ties that contrast to the Christian story of Eve, which she says brought sin into the world, and to the Haudenosaunee story of Sky Woman, who brought hope and growth. The episode describes these differences in general terms rather than as a systematic list, so the question asks the student to find and restate them.

*Ratings.* Answerable: yes. Clear: yes. Leading: no. Look up (the question asks the student to restate what the episode "describes").

**Q3.** Why does it matter to recognize and preserve the stories of people who have been overlooked or erased from history, and how does Eaton say a museum can recover them?

*Answer.* Eaton says that recovery matters because "They aren't erased anymore. Here they are right here." She describes starting with a single clue, "that little sparkle of a person," which can lead to "a census record" that shows where someone lived. The museum will show these people through "little doors that open up," so visitors can meet a story even when little is known about the person. Her reason is that "if we learn their story and we tell their story, then they're not erased because if they're in our memories, then they're still here."

*Ratings.* Answerable: yes. Clear: yes. Leading: no. Think.

**Q4.** According to Eaton, Gage left Anthony and Stanton in 1890 because she wanted equal rights rather than only the vote. Explain the two reasons Eaton gives for the break. How do those reasons connect to Gage's defense of Haudenosaunee sovereignty?

*Answer.* Eaton's first reason is the Temperance Union, which "wanted to create an amendment to the Constitution that would create Christianity as the state religion," and Gage had long supported "the freedom of religion and separation of church and state." The second reason is that the Southern Christian Democrats "had made a deal with northern white women" to keep Black people out of the polls once women had the vote. Eaton's account is that equal rights were the goal, "But Matilda wanted equal rights, and that was actually a different goal" from the vote, while the two reasons were the church issue and the Black vote. The episode does not connect either reason to Haudenosaunee sovereignty; it says only that Gage "speaks about rights for the Haudenosaunee," so the third part of the question is only partly answerable.

*Ratings.* Answerable: partly. Clear: yes. Leading: yes (mild): the premise states one reason, and the next sentence asks for two. Think.

**Q5.** Eaton presents Gage's comparison of Jigonsaseh and Eve as evidence that "their stories have formed their society." Explain the argument. What would a historian need, from Gage's writing or from the listed resources, to show that stories shaped laws and social roles in each society?

*Answer.* Eaton's argument is that "One society held that women brought sin and death into the world, and they followed the path of patriarchy, and the other society held that women brought life and peace and hope, and they followed the path of matriarchy." From this she concludes that "their stories have formed their society," and that "Matilda wrote a book about the evils of the Christian Church." The episode does not say what would show that stories shaped laws and social roles, so the second half of the question can be answered only in part. The listed resources are not in the episode, and I did not use them, per the instruction to use only the transcript.

*Ratings.* Answerable: partly. Clear: yes. Leading: no. Think.

**Suggestion, Episode 11.** Split Question 4. Keep the request for the two reasons, which the episode gives, and drop the request to connect them to sovereignty, which the episode does not draw. The premise's single "equal rights" reason should be stated as the goal, not as one of the reasons.
