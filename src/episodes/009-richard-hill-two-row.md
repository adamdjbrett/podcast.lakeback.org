## Show notes

In this episode, Adam DJ Brett, International Research Associate at the American Indian Law Alliance, discusses the historical, cultural, and spiritual significance of Onondaga Lake and the Great Law of Peace with Tuscarora artist, historian, and educator Richard W. Hill Sr. Hill reflects on how the Great Law functions as a worldview and an active framework for building relationships, cultivating peace, and maintaining responsibility toward the Earth. The conversation also explores the role of wampum in preserving Indigenous knowledge across generations, and how it shapes diplomacy and treaty relationships. Also examined are the tensions between Haudenosaunee understandings of land as sacred and interconnected, and colonial systems that prioritize economic development and property valuations. Hill emphasizes the importance of recovering Indigenous knowledge, reconsidering relationships with the natural world, and addressing environmental degradation through principles founded in reciprocity and responsibility.

The conversation then turns to the Two Row Wampum and its significance to Haudenosaunee treaty relationships, sovereignty, and cultural continuity. Hill discusses the metaphor of the canoe and ship traveling alongside one another, emphasizing mutual respect, noninterference, and the preservation of distinct cultural traditions. The conversation examines the role of wampum in treaty-making, the responsibilities associated with land and water, and the limitations of relying on colonial governments to uphold treaty relationships. Hill also reflects on land reclamation as a restoration of sacred relationships rather than simply a matter of property ownership. Throughout, he emphasizes the importance of environmental responsibility, intergenerational knowledge, and cultivating meaningful relationships to support the healing of Onondaga Lake and the broader natural world.

## Discussion questions

1. How does Richard W. Hill Sr. describe the Great Law of Peace as more than a written law? What role do relationships, personal reflection, and collective responsibility play in his understanding of peace?
2. What significance does wampum hold in Haudenosaunee culture, diplomacy, and treaty relationships? How does Hill distinguish the knowledge preserved through wampum and oral traditions from written colonial records?
3. Hill contrasts Haudenosaunee understandings of the Earth as sacred with colonial and industrial approaches that emphasize economic values and developments. How does this contrast help explain the relationship between treaty rights, Indigenous sovereignty, and environmental justice?
4. How does Hill interpret the Two Row Wampum’s metaphor of the canoe and the ship traveling together? What does this metaphor suggest?
5. Hill argues that treaties are not simply documents or wampum belts, but relationships that require ongoing respect, trust, communication, and responsibility. How does this understanding differ from conventional “western” legal or governmental approaches to treaties? What challenges arise when treaty partners no longer maintain those relationships?
6. How does Hill connect land reclamation and environmental restoration to Haudenosaunee cultural and spiritual responsibilities? What does his perspective suggest about the difference between returning land as property and restoring relationships with the Earth?
